\section{Setup}

Take a density field $\delta_{2N}$ sampled on a periodic cubic grid with
$2N$ points per side and spacing $\Delta x_f = L/(2N)$. The
\emph{restriction} operator $\R$ replaces each $2\times2\times2$ block of
fine cells (or $2\times2$ in 2D) by its arithmetic mean, producing
$\delta_N = \R\,\delta_{2N}$ on a grid of $N$ points per side and spacing
$\Delta x_c = L/N = 2\Delta x_f$. Each output cell is the volume average
of the eight (in 3D; four in 2D) fine cells inside it, which makes the
restriction \emph{locally} mass conserving: the mass in any coarse cell
$[I,J,K]$ equals the sum of the masses in its child fine cells,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-mass}
  (1 + \delta_{N,IJK})\,\Delta x_c^3
  \;=\;\!\!\!\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\,\mathrm{block}(I,J,K)}\!\!\!
        (1 + \delta_{2N,ijk})\,\Delta x_f^3,
\end{equation}
where the block contains the eight fine cells $\{(2I+a,2J+b,2K+c)\}_{a,b,c\in\{0,1\}}$.
Because $\Delta x_c^3 = 8\Delta x_f^3$, equation~(\ref{eq:local-mass})
reduces to $\delta_{N,IJK} = \tfrac{1}{8}\sum_{\rm block}\delta_{2N}$,
i.e.\ block averaging. Summing (\ref{eq:local-mass}) over all coarse
cells recovers global mass conservation as an immediate consequence.

The questions addressed here:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How is the Fourier transform of $\delta_N$ related to that of
      $\delta_{2N}$?
\item In first-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (1LPT), how does
      the particle displacement field $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$ change
      when computed from $\delta_N$ rather than $\delta_{2N}$?
\item How does the deformation tensor $\partial_i\Psi_j^{(1)}$ --- and
      the second-order LPT (2LPT) source built from it --- differ
      between the two grids?
\item What goes wrong if one instead restricts the displacement
      potential $\phi^{(1)}$ and then differentiates?
\end{enumerate}

All numerical illustrations are 2D for ease of visualization, with
$N=64$, $2N=128$, and box length $L=1$. The fine-grid density
$\delta_{2N}$ is a synthetic Gaussian random field generated by drawing
a unit-variance white noise field $\mu(\mathbf q)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$
in real space, taking its Fourier transform, multiplying by $\sqrt{P(k)}$
with a toy power spectrum $P(k)\propto k^n$ (we use $n=\toyn$ in the
figures below, chosen as the proper 2D analog of the high-$k$ slope of
$\Lambda$CDM --- see the dimensional argument in
\S\ref{sec:cdm}), setting the
$\mathbf k=\mathbf 0$ mode (the spatial mean) to zero, and transforming
back. This is a placeholder spectrum chosen for illustration --- it is
not the physical $\Lambda$CDM matter power spectrum --- but it has
enough small-scale power for the window and alias effects of restriction to
be visible (their absolute size depends on the slope $n$ chosen --- a
shallower spectrum makes them more pronounced). Zeroing the $\mathbf k=\mathbf 0$ mode makes
$\delta_{2N}$ have exactly zero mean on the grid by construction.

Throughout these notes the \emph{power spectrum} $P(\mathbf k)$ of a
statistically homogeneous random field $\delta(\mathbf x)$ is defined
by the two-point correlator of its Fourier modes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pk-def}
  \bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  \;=\; (2\pi)^d\,\delta^D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\,P(\mathbf k)
\end{equation}
where $\delta^D$ denotes the $d$-dimensional Dirac delta function and
$d$ is the spatial dimension (we use $d=2$ in our numerical
illustrations and $d=3$ when stating cosmological results).
Equivalently, on a periodic box of volume $V$ with discrete modes,
\begin{equation*}
  \bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  = V\,\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}\,P(\mathbf k),
\end{equation*}
where $\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}$ is the Kronecker delta.
Statistical isotropy further reduces $P(\mathbf k)\to P(|\mathbf k|)$. We will use $P_N$, $P_{2N}$, etc.\
for the spectra of the corresponding density fields ($\delta_N$,
$\delta_{2N}$); volume factors are suppressed when only ratios
$P_N/P_{2N}$ appear.

\section{Restriction in real and Fourier space}\label{sec:fourier}

Restriction is a linear operation. To see this in Fourier space,
think of it in two pieces: a \emph{convolution} of the fine-grid
samples with a small averaging kernel, followed by a $2{:}1$
\emph{subsampling} that keeps every other point. (For a self-contained
review of the Fourier-analysis tools used in this section ---
sampling, the Nyquist limit, aliasing, the convolution theorem ---
see \notefft.)

A note on notation: $\delta_{2N}$, $y$, and $\delta_N$ are
\emph{sequences} --- maps from integer grid indices to real numbers,
e.g.\ $\delta_{2N}:\mathbb Z^3/(2N\mathbb Z)^3\to\mathbb R$ with
$\delta_{2N,ijk}$ the value at index $(i,j,k)$. They are not functions
of a continuous spatial variable. The continuous field
$\delta(\mathbf x)$ enters only as the underlying object the grid
samples, and the kernel $h(x)$ versus its discrete weights $h_n$
illustrate the same distinction. Throughout, subscripted indices
($m$, $n$, $I$, $ijk$, $IJK$) tag sequence entries, while $\mathbf x$,
$\mathbf q$, $\mathbf k$ are continuous arguments.

The discrete convolution is the grid analogue of the familiar
continuous convolution
$(h*f)(x)=\int h(x-x')f(x')\,dx'$: replace the integral by a sum and
the continuous kernel $h(x)$ by a finite list of weights $h_n$. For
each output position $m$, the convolved value is a weighted sum of
nearby input samples,
\begin{equation}
  y_m \;=\; \sum_n h_n\,\delta_{2N,m+n}.
\end{equation}
The list $\{h_n\}$ is called a \emph{kernel}; for our restriction
operation it is the simplest possible smoothing kernel, an equal-weight
average over two adjacent fine cells.

In 1D, define the kernel\footnote{The integer $n$ here is just a
position label on the fine grid, in units of the fine spacing $\Delta x_f$:
$n=0$ refers to the sample at position $x=0$, $n=1$ to the sample at
$x=\Delta x_f$, and so on.}
\begin{equation}
  h_n \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tfrac12 & n=0,\\
    \tfrac12 & n=1,\\
    0 & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Plugging this into the convolution formula gives
$y_m = \tfrac12\bigl(\delta_{2N,m} + \delta_{2N,m+1}\bigr)$,
the moving average of two adjacent fine samples (defined on the same
fine grid as $\delta_{2N}$). Subsampling means we then keep only the
even-indexed entries:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-restrict}
  \delta_{N,I} \;=\; y_{2I}
              \;=\; \tfrac12\bigl(\delta_{2N,2I} + \delta_{2N,2I+1}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Equation~(\ref{eq:discrete-restrict}) is exactly $2{:}1$ block averaging:
the coarse cell with index $I$ is the mean of the two fine cells with
indices $2I$ and $2I+1$. The factor $\tfrac12$ in $h$ is the
normalization that conserves cell averages (sums to one), so a constant
field is mapped to itself.

In 3D the same construction applies separately along each axis. The
kernel \emph{factorizes} into a tensor product,
\begin{equation}
  h_{3{\rm D},\,abc} \;=\; h_a\,h_b\,h_c
                     \;=\; \begin{cases}
                              1/8 & a,b,c\in\{0,1\},\\
                              0   & \text{otherwise},
                            \end{cases}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ a $2\!\times\!2\!\times\!2$ array of value $1/8$ (which is
$1/8 = (1/2)^3$, the per-cell weight that makes the kernel sum to
unity). The convolution is
\begin{equation}
  y_{\ell,m,n} \;=\; \sum_{a,b,c} h_{3{\rm D},\,abc}\,
                    \delta_{2N,\,\ell+a,\,m+b,\,n+c},
\end{equation}
a moving average of the eight fine samples in a $2\!\times\!2\!\times\!2$
window. Subsampling $2{:}1$ in each direction then keeps only the
even-indexed entries, so
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{N,IJK} \;=\; y_{2I,2J,2K}
                  \;=\; \frac{1}{8}\!\!\sum_{a,b,c\in\{0,1\}}\!\!
                        \delta_{2N,\,2I+a,\,2J+b,\,2K+c},
\end{equation}
exactly the local mass-conservation formula~(\ref{eq:local-mass}). The
2D version is the same construction with $h_{2{\rm D}}[a,b]=1/4$ on a
$2\!\times\!2$ block. To name the two steps explicitly: forming
$y_{\ell,m,n}$ via the convolution is the \emph{smoothing} step (it
stays on the fine grid and replaces each fine sample by a moving
average of its $2{\times}2{\times}2$ neighbourhood), and keeping only
the entries $y_{2I,2J,2K}$ is the \emph{subsampling} step (it discards
every other sample along each axis to land on the coarse grid).

In the continuous-volume limit (many fine samples per coarse cell, so
$\Delta x_f\to 0$ at fixed $\Delta x_c$) this discrete kernel approaches
a real-space top-hat of width $\Delta x_c$ along each axis (one coarse
cell), followed by point sampling at the coarse-cell centres.

\paragraph{Smoothing step in Fourier space.}
The continuous-$k$ Fourier transform of the discrete kernel $h_n$
(discrete in samples $n$, continuous in $k$ --- not to be confused with
the finite-in/finite-out discrete Fourier transform computed by FFT
routines) is
\begin{equation}
  H(k)\;=\;\sum_n h_n\,e^{-i k n \Delta x_f}
       \;=\;\tfrac12\bigl(1 + e^{-ik\Delta x_f}\bigr)
       \;=\;e^{-ik\Delta x_f/2}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{k\Delta x_f}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
The factored form on the right separates two things. The cosine is the
Fourier transform of the \emph{centered} version of the same kernel
(weights $\tfrac12$ at $x=\pm\Delta x_f/2$), which is real and even
because that kernel is symmetric about the origin. The exponential
$e^{-ik\Delta x_f/2}$ is just the shift property of Fourier transforms
($f(x-s)\leftrightarrow e^{-iks}\hat f(k)$) telling us that our kernel,
sitting at $\{0,\Delta x_f\}$, is offset from the centered one by
$\Delta x_f/2$ --- the center of mass of the two samples we're
averaging.

More generally, the \emph{cell window} $W(\mathbf k)$ is just the
Fourier transform of whatever real-space kernel is used in the
smoothing step of restriction. For \emph{any} averaging operation
written as a convolution
\begin{align}
  y(\mathbf x) &\;=\; \int h(\mathbf x - \mathbf x')\,f(\mathbf x')\,d^d x'
                 \quad\text{(continuous)},\\
  y_{\mathbf m} &\;=\; \sum_{\mathbf n} h_{\mathbf n}\,f_{\mathbf m + \mathbf n}
                 \quad\text{(discrete)},
\end{align}
the cell window is
\begin{equation}
  W(\mathbf k) \;\equiv\; \hat h(\mathbf k),
\end{equation}
where $\hat h$ is the Fourier transform of $h$ (the continuous-$k$
Fourier transform discussed earlier, in the discrete-kernel case). Operationally, $W(\mathbf k)$ is the per-mode
\emph{transfer function} of the smoothing step alone: the smoothed
field's Fourier coefficient at mode $\mathbf k$ is
$\hat y(\mathbf k) = W(\mathbf k)\,\hat f(\mathbf k)$. Different choices
of cell averaging (top-hat, triangular cloud-in-cell, Gaussian, etc.)
produce different $W$, but in every case the same statement holds: $W$
is the Fourier transform of the kernel, and it tells you how strongly
each Fourier mode is multiplied during smoothing. The full restriction
then composes this with the alias sum from subsampling, as in
(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}).

We adopt the convention of taking the Fourier transform of the
\emph{centered} kernel as our cell window, so that $W$ is real and we
do not have to carry the phase factor around. The original kernel
$h_n=\tfrac12$ at $n=0,1$ has its center of mass at $n=\tfrac12$, i.e.\
half a fine cell ($\Delta x_f/2$) to the right of the origin; recentering it
to $n=\pm\tfrac12$ shifts the kernel by $-\Delta x_f/2$ in real space
and removes the corresponding phase factor
$e^{-ik_a\Delta x_f/2}$ from its Fourier transform. The shift is a
pure translation of the kernel: it does not change $|W|$, and so does
not change which Fourier modes are suppressed by what amount. With this convention,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Wcell}
  W(\mathbf k) \;=\; \prod_a \cos\!\left(\frac{k_a\Delta x_f}{2}\right)
  \qquad (a\in\{x,y,z\}\text{ in 3D},\; \{x,y\}\text{ in 2D}).
\end{equation}
$W$ is signed real: it can take negative values when one of the
arguments $k_a\Delta x_f/2$ exceeds $\pi/2$, i.e.\ outside the
fine-mode region. Inside the fine-mode region $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$,
all arguments lie in $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ and $W\ge 0$.

For comparison, the centered \emph{continuous} top-hat of width
$\Delta x_c=2\Delta x_f$ has Fourier transform
\begin{equation}
  W_{\rm cont}(\mathbf k) \;=\; \prod_a \mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{k_a\Delta x_c}{2}\right),
  \qquad \mathrm{sinc}(x)\equiv \sin x/x,
\end{equation}
also signed real (with negative side lobes outside the central peak).
The two definitions are now on equal footing: both are FTs of centered
kernels, no absolute values.

As functions of $\mathbf k$, both $W$ and $W_{\rm cont}$ are defined on
all of $\mathbb R^d$ (the cosines and sincs accept any real argument,
with $\mathrm{sinc}(0)=1$). In our setup the wavenumber is not really
a continuous variable, though: in a periodic box of side $L$ the
allowed modes form the discrete reciprocal lattice $\mathbf k =(2\pi/L)\mathbf m$
with $\mathbf m\in\mathbb Z^d$, and on the fine grid the distinct modes
are further restricted to one period of the lattice, conventionally
$|m_a|\le N$ (equivalently $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$). The bare cosine
$\cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$ has period $4\pi/\Delta x_f$ in $k_a$, so as
$k_a$ sweeps the fine-mode range $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$ the argument
$k_a\Delta x_f/2$ sweeps $[-\pi/2,+\pi/2]$ --- a span of $\pi$, half
the $2\pi$ period of cosine. Over that half-period the cosine takes
the values $0\to 1\to 0$: it equals $1$ at $k_a=0$ and falls
monotonically (in $|k_a|$) to $0$ at the fine Nyquist
$|k_a|=\pi/\Delta x_f$. The vanishing of $W$ at the fine Nyquist is
not a coincidence: the fine-Nyquist mode is the alternating pattern
$(+1,-1,+1,-1,\dots)$, and averaging any two adjacent fine samples of
that pattern gives zero, so the smoothing step annihilates this mode
exactly. $W_{\rm cont}$, by contrast, is non-periodic --- it just
decays with side lobes as $|k|\to\infty$.

The periodicity distinction is largely academic for our purposes. In
the periodic-box setup, $\mathbf k$ is not a continuous variable: it
takes only the discrete reciprocal-lattice values
$\mathbf k=(2\pi/L)\mathbf m$ for integer $\mathbf m$, and on a fine
grid of $2N$ points per side the distinct discrete modes are
restricted to one period $|m_a|\le N$, equivalently
$|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$ (the fine-mode region). So in practice we
never evaluate $W$ outside that fine-mode region, even though $W$ as
a mathematical function is defined on all of $\mathbb R^d$. The
fine-mode region $|m_a|\le N$ is one Brillouin zone of the mode
lattice ($2N$ distinct modes), but only half of $W$'s own period in
$k_a$ (which is $4\pi/\Delta x_f$, equivalent to $|m_a|\le 2N$). So
$W$'s periodicity never even gets exercised --- all our evaluations
stay within half of one cycle of $W$.

$W$ and $W_{\rm cont}$ agree to leading order in $k\Delta x_f$ but
diverge near the coarse \emph{Nyquist wavenumber} (the highest
wavenumber a grid of spacing $\Delta x_c$ can resolve,
$k_{\rm Ny,N} \equiv \pi/\Delta x_c$); in our 2-sample restriction,
equation~(\ref{eq:Wcell}) is the exact one. Figure~\ref{fig:window}
shows the two kernels side by side in real and Fourier space. At the
coarse Nyquist they read $W=\cos(\pi/4)\approx 0.707$ and
$W_{\rm cont}=\mathrm{sinc}(\pi/2)=2/\pi\approx 0.637$, an
$\sim\!11\%$ disagreement.

\paragraph{Subsampling step in Fourier space.}
Now the second operation: throw away every other fine sample to land
on the coarse grid. The price is that some fine-grid Fourier modes
become indistinguishable from each other once we only look at every
other point. To see why, evaluate two pure plane waves of fine-grid
wavenumbers $k$ and $k+2\pi/\Delta x_c$ at the coarse-grid positions
$x_I = I\Delta x_c$:
\begin{equation}
  e^{i(k+2\pi/\Delta x_c)\,x_I}
  \;=\; e^{ik\,x_I}\;e^{i2\pi I}
  \;=\; e^{ik\,x_I},
\end{equation}
since $e^{i2\pi I}=1$ for any integer $I$. The two waves give exactly
the same sequence of values on the coarse grid, so the coarse grid
cannot tell them apart --- on the coarse grid, the higher-frequency
mode is indistinguishable from the lower-frequency one. This is what
``\emph{aliasing}'' means: a high-frequency mode that the coarse grid
cannot resolve gets misidentified as a lower-frequency mode it can. The shift between aliased modes is
$\Delta k_{\rm alias}=2\pi/\Delta x_c=\pi/\Delta x_f$ (recall
$\Delta x_c=2\Delta x_f$). Note that this alias spacing along each
axis is exactly the fine-grid Nyquist wavenumber $k_{\rm Ny,2N}$:
every coarse-grid mode at $k_c$ pairs with the fine-grid mode at
$k_c \pm k_{\rm Ny,2N}$ (the two signs give the same fine mode, since
they differ by $2k_{\rm Ny,2N}$ which is one fine-grid period in $k$).
Choosing whichever sign keeps the result inside the fine-mode region,
the alias lands at $k_c-\mathrm{sgn}(k_c)\,k_{\rm Ny,2N}$ --- so its
sign is opposite to $k_c$, and its magnitude lies in the outer half
$k_{\rm Ny,N}\le|k_a|\le k_{\rm Ny,2N}$, the band between coarse and
fine Nyquist. The two are
indistinguishable after subsampling. Figure~\ref{fig:alias} shows the
2D version of this pairing: in 2D, each coarse mode receives
contributions from $2\times 2 = 4$ fine modes (one principal and three
aliases), one in each quadrant of the fine-mode region. In 3D the
count becomes $2\times 2\times 2 = 8$: one principal and seven
aliases, one in each of the seven other octants.

Concretely, for every coarse mode $\mathbf k_c$ inside the resolved
coarse-mode region ($|k_{c,a}|\le\pi/\Delta x_c$ for each axis), there
are exactly two fine-grid modes per axis that fall inside the fine-mode
region and alias onto it: one at $k_{c,a}$ itself (the ``principal''
mode, already in the coarse region) and one at
$k_{c,a}-\mathrm{sgn}(k_{c,a})\,\pi/\Delta x_f$, which lies in the
outer wing of the fine zone with sign opposite to $k_{c,a}$. Either way, the alias partner has magnitude
$|k_a|\in[k_{\rm Ny,N},\,k_{\rm Ny,2N}]$, sitting beyond the coarse
Nyquist but inside the fine Nyquist. In 3D this gives $2\times 2\times 2 = 8$
fine modes per coarse mode (one principal + seven aliases),
enumerated by which axis is shifted or not.
Calling that bookkeeping vector $\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^3$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restrict-fourier}
  \hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c)
  \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^3}
        W(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\,
        \hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f).
\end{equation}
The cell window $W$ from~(\ref{eq:Wcell}) multiplies each contribution
because the convolution step happened first, before subsampling. The
$\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$ term is the \emph{principal} contribution: it is
the cell-window-suppressed value of $\hat\delta_{2N}$ at $\mathbf k_c$
itself. The seven $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ terms are the
\emph{aliases}: they carry fine-grid power from \emph{outside} the
coarse Nyquist back into the coarse mode $\mathbf k_c$, where it is no
longer separable from the genuine low-frequency content. Appendix
\ref{app:aliassum} gives a derivation of (\ref{eq:restrict-fourier})
that tracks the Fourier-convention prefactor explicitly.

A note on the shifts $\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ in
(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}): for $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ the argument
$\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ generally lies \emph{outside}
the fine-mode region $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$. That is harmless
because $\hat\delta_{2N}$ is periodic in $\mathbf k$ with period
$2\pi/\Delta x_f$ along each axis --- the standard consequence of
sampling a field on a grid with spacing $\Delta x_f$ (discrete in
real space $\Leftrightarrow$ periodic in Fourier space; see
\notefft \S``Nyquist frequency and aliasing,'' which
shows that on a grid of spacing $\Delta x_f$ one has
$e^{ik x_n}=e^{i(k-2\pi m/\Delta x_f)x_n}$, so wavenumbers differing
by $2\pi/\Delta x_f$ are indistinguishable on the grid, i.e.\
$\hat\delta_{2N}$ has period $2\pi/\Delta x_f$ along each axis). So the shifted argument is just a relabeling
of a fine-grid mode that already lives inside the fine-mode region,
and one can equivalently use $-\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ in place of
$+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$. The window $W(\mathbf k)$, by contrast,
is a smooth function on all of $\mathbb R^d$ and is evaluated at the
shifted point literally, not periodically.

The two pieces play complementary roles. The convolution
(\ref{eq:Wcell}) acts as a smoothing filter: it suppresses high-$k$
power inside the resolved coarse-mode region smoothly, with $W=1$ at
$\mathbf k=\mathbf 0$ and $W\!\to\!0$ at the corners of the fine-mode
region. The subsampling step keeps every other fine sample in each
direction; equivalently, in real space it multiplies by a sum of
equally-spaced spikes that pick out only the kept samples. In Fourier
space, this folds the fine spectrum onto the coarse one. Picture the
fine spectrum living inside the fine-mode region $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$;
after subsampling, the new fundamental period along each axis is half
as wide, $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_c$. To get the new spectrum, you overlay
the fine spectrum on itself, shifted by every integer multiple of the
new period $2\pi/\Delta x_c=\pi/\Delta x_f$ along each axis, and sum
within one new fundamental cell. Inside the larger fine-mode region
there are two ``shift or no shift'' choices per axis, so in 3D each
coarse-grid mode receives $2\times 2\times 2 = 8$ overlapping
contributions --- one principal ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$, the unshifted
copy) and seven aliases (shifted copies). These are exactly the eight
terms in (\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}). Restriction is therefore not a clean
band-limiting operation --- it is a smoothing step followed by a fold.
In particular, restriction is \emph{not} simply ``multiply by $W$ in
Fourier space.'' Multiplication by $W$ is only the smoothing step,
which produces the principal ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$) term in
(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}); the seven alias terms come from the
subsampling. The two pictures coincide only in the special case where
$\hat\delta_{2N}$ has no power above the coarse Nyquist
($|k_a|>\pi/\Delta x_c$), so that all $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ terms
vanish identically.

The same alias structure shows up at the level of power spectra. For
the smoothing step alone, the real-space convolution with $h$
becomes multiplication by $W$ in Fourier space (convolution theorem),
$\hat y(\mathbf k) = W(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k)$, so the smoothed-field power spectrum is
$P_y(\mathbf k) = |W(\mathbf k)|^2\,P_{2N}(\mathbf k)$ --- the standard
``convolution multiplies $P$ by $|W|^2$'' result. For the full
restriction, take the squared modulus of (\ref{eq:restrict-fourier})
and ensemble-average. Writing $\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}\equiv\mathbf
k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ for brevity,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl\langle|\hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c)|^2\bigr\rangle
  \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf n,\mathbf n'} W(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n})\,
        W^*(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n'})\,
        \bigl\langle\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n})\,
                    \hat\delta_{2N}^*(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n'})\bigr\rangle.
\end{equation}
For a statistically homogeneous field, distinct Fourier modes are
uncorrelated:
\begin{equation*}
\langle\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta_{2N}^*(\mathbf k')\rangle
\propto P_{2N}(\mathbf k)\,\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}
\end{equation*}
on the fine-grid lattice. Since $\mathbf n,\mathbf n'\in\{0,1\}^3$, every component of
$\mathbf n-\mathbf n'$ lies in $\{-1,0,+1\}$, so the shifts
$\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}$ and $\mathbf k_{\mathbf n'}$ differ by
$\pi(\mathbf n-\mathbf n')/\Delta x_f$, with each component in
$\{-\pi/\Delta x_f,\,0,\,+\pi/\Delta x_f\}$. The only multiple of the
fine-grid period $2\pi/\Delta x_f$ in that set is $0$, which forces
$\mathbf n=\mathbf n'$; for $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf n'$ the two shifts
correspond to distinct fine modes and the cross-terms vanish. Only
the $\mathbf n=\mathbf n'$ diagonal survives, giving the alias sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pk-alias}
  P_N(\mathbf k_c) \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^3}
        \bigl|W(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\bigr|^2\,
        P_{2N}(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f).
\end{equation}
(Gaussianity is not needed; homogeneity alone gives the diagonal
two-point function in Fourier space.)
The $\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$ term is the clean ``$|W|^2 P$'' smoothing
contribution; the seven $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ terms add fine-grid
power from beyond the coarse Nyquist, weighted by $|W|^2$ evaluated at
those higher wavenumbers --- a positive, incoherent contamination of
the coarse spectrum.

Figure~\ref{fig:density} compares $\delta_{2N}$ and $\delta_N$ in real
space (panels~1 and~2) and shows the Fourier amplitude map of the fine
field (panel~3). Panel~4 plots the per-mode amplitude ratio
$|\hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c)|/|\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)|$ against
$|\mathbf k_c|$ on a linear-$k$, log-$|$ratio$|$ scale. Three things
are overlaid: the measured ratios (blue), the exact 2D cell window
$W^2(k_x,k_y)$ evaluated at the same modes (green), and the 1D proxy
$\cos^2(k\Delta x_f/2)$ (red curve). The blue and green points
together expose the two ingredients of the alias-sum formula: the
green points show what restriction would produce if there were no
aliasing (just the principal-mode multiplication by $W$), and the blue
points include the alias contributions on top.

Note that $W$ is direction-dependent: at fixed $|\mathbf k|$, modes
along an axis and modes along the diagonal feel different cell-window
suppression. For example at the coarse Nyquist along an axis,
$\mathbf k_c=(\pi/\Delta x_c, 0)$, the principal suppression is
$W=\cos(\pi/4)\cdot 1 \approx 0.707$, but at the corner of the coarse
zone $\mathbf k_c=(\pi/\Delta x_c, \pi/\Delta x_c)$ it drops to
$W=\cos^2(\pi/4)=1/2$ (and in 3D the cube corner gives
$\cos^3(\pi/4)\approx 0.354$). The spread at fixed $|\mathbf k|$ is
small inside the coarse-mode region: writing $u=|\mathbf
k|\Delta x_f/2$, the extremes are $W=\cos u$ (axis-aligned),
$W=\cos^2(u/\sqrt 2)$ (face-diagonal in 2D), and $W=\cos^3(u/\sqrt 3)$
(body-diagonal in 3D), which at the coarse Nyquist ($u=\pi/4$) give
$0.707$, $0.722$, and $0.727$ respectively --- a relative spread of
only $\sim$2--3\%. At each $|\mathbf k|$ the green points therefore
form a narrow band rather than a single value. The 1D proxy
$\cos^2(|\mathbf k|\Delta x_f/2)$ tracks the axis-aligned edge of this
band at low $|\mathbf k|$, but past the coarse Nyquist axis-aligned
modes are absent and only diagonal directions survive, so the proxy
falls below the actual band. The much larger blue
scatter on top of the green band is the alias contamination, which
grows toward the coarse Nyquist where high-$k$ fine-grid power
($|\mathbf k|>\pi/\Delta x_c$) is folded back in by subsampling.

Before turning to the spectrum dependence, it is useful to separate
the two ingredients of restriction. \emph{Pure subsampling} (no
block-averaging) folds high-$k$ power onto each coarse mode but
preserves total variance: writing the alias sum without $W$,
$P_{\rm sub}(\mathbf k_c) = \sum_{\mathbf n}P_{2N}(\mathbf k_c+\pi
\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)$, the integral over the coarse Brillouin zone
exactly tiles the fine zone,
\[
  \int_{\rm coarse\,BZ}\!P_{\rm sub}(\mathbf k_c)\,d^d k_c
  \;=\; \int_{\rm fine\,BZ}\!P_{2N}(\mathbf k)\,d^d k,
\]
because every fine mode falls into exactly one coarse mode under the
periodic identification. So pure subsampling \emph{redistributes}
power (preserves total variance) without changing it. \emph{Block
averaging}, by contrast, multiplies each fine-mode power by
$W^2(\mathbf k)\le 1$, so it can only \emph{remove} variance. The
full restriction $\R$ (block-average then subsample) combines both
effects: the total variance of $\delta_N$ is
$\sum_{\mathbf n}W^2 P_{2N}$ summed over the coarse Brillouin zone,
which is bounded above by the fine variance and equals it iff
$\sum_{\mathbf n}W^2(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)=1$ for all
$\mathbf k_c$ --- the Parseval identity derived below.

The amount of aliasing depends on the input power spectrum slope $n$
(with $P(k)\propto k^n$). The alias-sum formula~(\ref{eq:Pk-alias}) gives
$P_N(\mathbf k_c) = \sum_{\mathbf n}W^2(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf
n/\Delta x_f)\,P_{2N}(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)$. The ratio
$P_N(\mathbf k_c)/P_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)$ then ranges over the following
limits:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{White noise} ($n=0$). Every alias mode carries the same
power as the principal, so
$P_N(\mathbf k_c)/P_{2N}(\mathbf k_c) =
\sum_{\bf n}W^2(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f) = 1$ at every $\mathbf k_c$.
To see this, recall that the $2\times 2$ box-average window factorizes,
$W(\mathbf k)=\prod_{a}\cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$, and the aliases
$\mathbf n$ are vectors with each component in $\{0,\pi/\Delta x_f\}$,
so the alias sum factorizes too:
\begin{align*}
\sum_{\mathbf n}W^2(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)
&= \prod_a\left[\cos^2\!\left(\tfrac{k_{c,a}\Delta x_f}{2}\right)
              +\cos^2\!\left(\tfrac{k_{c,a}\Delta x_f}{2}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right] \\
&= \prod_a\left[\cos^2 u_a + \sin^2 u_a\right] = 1,
\end{align*}
where $u_a=k_{c,a}\Delta x_f/2$. This identity is just Parseval for
the box-average kernel: real-space block averaging conserves total
variance, so the ratio cannot exceed $1$.
\item \textbf{Red spectra} ($n<0$). The alias modes at $\mathbf
k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ have lower power than the principal, so the ratio
drops toward $W^2(\mathbf k_c)$ at low/mid $|\mathbf k_c|$. The
steeper the spectrum, the cleaner the recovery: at $n=-5$ the
binned ratio sits on the $W^2$ band almost up to the coarse
Nyquist.
\item \textbf{Near the coarse Nyquist}, the dominant alias mode lands
at $|\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f|\sim|\mathbf k_c|$, so its power is
comparable to the principal regardless of $n$. The ratio is
inflated above $W^2$ here even for very steep spectra --- aliasing
near the cutoff is unavoidable in this estimator.
\end{itemize}
Note that the radially-binned ratio
$\langle P_N\rangle_{\rm bin}/\langle P_{2N}\rangle_{\rm bin}$ shown
in the bottom-middle panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:density} is the same
quantity, with the ensemble average replaced by a sum over modes in
the bin. The per-mode amplitude ratio plotted in the bottom-left panel
contains the same information \emph{plus} extra scatter from the
random magnitude of
each $\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)$ in the denominator: occasional
modes happen to draw a small principal amplitude, and dividing by
that small number blows the ratio up. This denominator-noise
contribution is independent of the alias size, so it does not shrink
with $n$.

This near-Nyquist aliasing is actually a sharper warning than just
``aliasing is bad here.'' For $n=0$ the cancellation is \emph{exact}
by Parseval ($\sum_{\mathbf n}W^2_{\rm aliases}=1$), and for
moderately red spectra the curves still pass through ratio
$\sim 1$ somewhere near the coarse Nyquist (visible in the
bottom-right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:density}). So the danger isn't
that $P_N$ looks visibly wrong --- it is that $P_N$ can look
\emph{right} (close to $P_{2N}$, or close to $W^2 P_{2N}$) while the
modes themselves are scrambled: \textbf{the principal-mode
information has been suppressed by $W^2$ and replaced by an
alias-weighted sum from $|\mathbf k|>\pi/\Delta x_c$}. The total variance per shell is
preserved, but the mode-by-mode content isn't recoverable. Agreement
of $P_N$ with $P_{2N}$ near the coarse Nyquist is therefore not
evidence that the restricted modes are clean; it can reflect
cancellation between $W^2$ suppression and alias contamination.

The practical conclusion is to ``work with the unrestricted fine
field'': don't restrict. Whatever quantity is needed (a power
spectrum, a derivative, an LPT source term), compute it on the $2N$
fine grid where those wavenumbers are well below the fine Nyquist and
aliasing is harmless, instead of on the $N$ coarse grid where the
same physical $k$ sits near the coarse Nyquist. The zoom-in
construction we adopt below (\S\ref{sec:zoom}) follows this principle
strictly: it never restricts a sampled density or potential. Instead
it uses a shared fine-grid white noise $\mu$ and constructs the
lo-res field by $k$-truncating $\mu$ at the coarse Nyquist and
convolving with the transfer function on the fine grid, in the manner
of MUSIC2~v2 / GRAFIC2 / monofonIC. The resulting band-limited
lo-res field carries the same low-$k$ modes as $\delta_{2N}$ with no
cell-window suppression and no alias contamination.

\section{1LPT displacement under restriction}\label{sec:psi}

\emph{Lagrangian perturbation theory} (LPT) describes structure
formation by following individual fluid elements through their
displacement from an initial uniform configuration. Each fluid element
is labelled by its initial (Lagrangian) position $\mathbf q$, and its
later (Eulerian) position is $\mathbf x(\mathbf q, t) = \mathbf q +
\boldsymbol\Psi(\mathbf q, t)$, where $\boldsymbol\Psi$ is the
displacement. To first order in the density contrast $\delta$, the
displacement $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpt-poisson}
  \nabla_q \cdot \boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}(\mathbf q) = -\delta_{\rm lin}(\mathbf q),
\end{equation}
which is mass conservation kept only to first order in
$\delta$ (the full nonlinear continuity equation reduces to this when
products of small perturbations are dropped). $\delta_{\rm lin}$ here
is the linear-theory density contrast at some reference time. (See
\notecosmoic for the full derivation and the
extension to higher orders.)

The growing-mode solution to (\ref{eq:lpt-poisson}) (the mode whose
amplitude increases with time, in contrast to the decaying mode) is
curl-free, so we can write $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)} =
-\nabla_q\phi^{(1)}$ for a scalar potential $\phi^{(1)}$. Substituting
into~(\ref{eq:lpt-poisson}) gives the Poisson-like equation
$\nabla_q^2\phi^{(1)} = \delta_{\rm lin}$, where the Laplacian is taken
with respect to the Lagrangian coordinate $\mathbf q$. We call
$\phi^{(1)}$ the \emph{displacement potential} (or Lagrangian
potential). It is not the gravitational potential $\Phi$, which obeys
$\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\bar\rho a^2\delta$ and has different units; the
two are proportional in linear theory but solve equations with
different right-hand sides.

In Fourier space the Laplacian becomes multiplication by $-k^2$, so the
Poisson equation $\nabla_q^2\phi^{(1)} = \delta_{\rm lin}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  -k^2\,\hat\phi^{(1)}(\mathbf k) \;=\; \hat\delta_{\rm lin}(\mathbf k)
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \hat\phi^{(1)}(\mathbf k) \;=\; -\frac{\hat\delta_{\rm lin}(\mathbf k)}{k^2}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)} = -\nabla_q\phi^{(1)}$
(gradient becomes $i\mathbf k$ in Fourier),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi-kernel}
  \hat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{(1)}(\mathbf k)
  \;=\; -i\mathbf k\,\hat\phi^{(1)}(\mathbf k)
  \;=\; -i\mathbf k\cdot\left(-\frac{\hat\delta_{\rm lin}(\mathbf k)}{k^2}\right)
  \;=\; +\frac{i\mathbf k}{k^2}\,\hat\delta_{\rm lin}(\mathbf k).
\end{equation}
As a sanity check, the Poisson constraint is satisfied identically:
$i\mathbf k\!\cdot\!\hat{\boldsymbol\Psi}^{(1)} = (i\mathbf k)\!\cdot\!(i\mathbf k\hat\delta_{\rm lin}/k^2) = -\hat\delta_{\rm lin}$, in agreement with~(\ref{eq:lpt-poisson}).

The peculiar velocity (the deviation from Hubble flow) follows from
$\mathbf v^{(1)} = aHf\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$, where $a$ is the
cosmological scale factor, $H=\dot a/a$ is the Hubble rate, and
$f\equiv d\ln D/d\ln a$ is the linear growth rate ($D$ being the
linear growth factor). Since $aHf$ is a spatially constant prefactor,
the same maps and fractional restriction error in
Figure~\ref{fig:psi} also describe $\mathbf v^{(1)}$, only with
different units.

The kernel $i\mathbf k/k^2$ in (\ref{eq:psi-kernel}) has magnitude
$1/|\mathbf k|$, so $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$ receives most of its
amplitude from large-scale modes. We expect restriction --- which
modifies the field near the coarse Nyquist scale --- to leave
$\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$, and therefore $\mathbf v^{(1)}$, nearly
unchanged. Figure~\ref{fig:psi} confirms this. We compare two ways of constructing $\Psi_x^{(1)}$ on
the coarse grid:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] solve (\ref{eq:psi-kernel}) on the fine grid for
      $\boldsymbol\Psi^{(1)}_{2N}$, then restrict each component to
      the coarse grid: $\R\,\Psi^{(1)}_{x,2N}$.
\item[(b)] restrict $\delta$ first, $\delta_N = \R\,\delta_{2N}$, then
      solve (\ref{eq:psi-kernel}) on the coarse grid:
      $\Psi^{(1)}_x[\delta_N]$.
\end{itemize}
The $\R$ operator is the same in both cases (the block-averaging
restriction defined in~\eqref{eq:discrete-restrict}); only the order
in which it is applied relative to the Poisson solve differs.
Figure~\ref{fig:psi} shows the two maps, their difference, and a
histogram of the per-cell difference. On the colour scale of the field
itself the maps are indistinguishable; the difference panel uses a
rescaled colour range about an order of magnitude smaller; and the
histogram has an RMS of a few percent of
$\mathrm{RMS}(\Psi^{(1)}_{x,(a)})$. Because $\mathbf v^{(1)}$ differs
from $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}$ only by the spatially constant factor
$aHf$, the same diagnostic applies to the velocity unchanged.

The residual is small but not exactly zero, and its sign and scale
dependence have a simple origin in the alias-sum formula. Both
constructions fold aliased fine-grid contributions into each coarse
mode $\mathbf k_c$, but with different prefactors. The kernel
magnitude $1/|\mathbf k|$ \emph{falls} with $|\mathbf k|$, and the
aliased modes live at high $|\mathbf k'| > |\mathbf k_c|$. So the
question is at which $k$ the kernel is evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(a)] The gradient acts on the fine grid, so each fine alias
        $\mathbf k' = \mathbf k_c + 2\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_c$ enters
        with kernel $i\mathbf k'/(k')^2$, magnitude $1/k'$ ---
        \textbf{small}, because $k'$ is large.
  \item[(b)] The gradient acts on the coarse grid, so the alias has
        already been folded onto $\mathbf k_c$ before the inverse
        Laplacian is applied, and it enters with kernel
        $i\mathbf k_c/k_c^{\,2}$, magnitude $1/k_c$ --- \textbf{larger},
        because $k_c < k'$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Construction (b) therefore amplifies aliases relative to (a)}:
near the coarse Nyquist the suppression factor $1/k'$ is replaced by
the larger $1/k_c$. At low $k_c$ the alias terms are negligible and
the two constructions agree. The
same prefactor difference appears in the table of \S\ref{sec:phi},
where it sets the alias entries of constructions (1) and (2). For
redder spectra the residual is small (a few percent at $n=\toyn$);
for bluer spectra it grows, and for white noise it is order unity.

\section{Deformation tensor and the 2LPT source}\label{sec:deformation}

The gradient of the displacement, $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j} \equiv \partial_j\Psi^{(1)}_i$
(the comma in the subscript denotes a partial derivative, $f_{,i}\equiv\partial f/\partial q_i$),
is the Lagrangian \emph{deformation tensor}. Its trace is the Poisson
constraint $\sum_i\Psi^{(1)}_{i,i} = -\delta_{\rm lin}$, and the velocity gradient
follows from $\partial_j v^{(1)}_i = aHf\,\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$. More importantly
for our purposes, the deformation tensor is the building block of the
2LPT source $S^{(2)}$, which appears as the right-hand side of a
second Poisson-like equation
$\nabla_q^2\phi^{(2)} = S^{(2)}$ for the second-order displacement
potential $\phi^{(2)}$ (with $\boldsymbol\Psi^{(2)} = -\nabla_q\phi^{(2)}$).
In any dimension the source is the second principal invariant of the
deformation tensor,\footnote{For a $3\times 3$ matrix $A$ the
\emph{principal invariants} are the coefficients of its characteristic
polynomial $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^3 - I_1\lambda^2 + I_2\lambda - I_3$:
$I_1 = \mathrm{tr}\, A$, $I_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}[(\mathrm{tr}\, A)^2 - \mathrm{tr}\,(A^2)] = \sum_{i<j}(A_{ii}A_{jj}-A_{ij}A_{ji})$,
and $I_3 = \det A$. They are ``invariants'' because they are unchanged
by rotations of the coordinate frame; equivalently, in terms of
eigenvalues, $I_1=\sum_i\lambda_i$, $I_2=\sum_{i<j}\lambda_i\lambda_j$,
$I_3=\prod_i\lambda_i$. For 1LPT the deformation tensor is symmetric
(it is the Hessian of $-\phi^{(1)}$), so $A_{ij}A_{ji}=(A_{ij})^2$ and
$I_2$ takes the form below.}
\begin{equation}
  S^{(2)}(\mathbf q) \;=\;
    \sum_{i<j}\!\Bigl[\,\Psi^{(1)}_{i,i}\,\Psi^{(1)}_{j,j}
                       \;-\;\bigl(\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}\bigr)^2\Bigr]
\end{equation}
(see \notecosmoic~\S2LPT for the derivation from the
Jacobian expansion). In 3D this gives three terms; in 2D it
reduces to the single off-diagonal invariant
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s2}
  S^{(2)}(\mathbf q) \;=\; \Psi^{(1)}_{x,x}\,\Psi^{(1)}_{y,y}
                          \;-\;\bigl(\Psi^{(1)}_{x,y}\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
which is just the determinant of the 2D deformation tensor.

The Fourier representation of the deformation tensor follows from
differentiating~(\ref{eq:psi-kernel}) once more: the partial derivative
$\partial_j$ becomes multiplication by $ik_j$ in Fourier space, so
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}}(\mathbf k)
    \;=\; ik_j\,\widehat{\Psi^{(1)}_i}(\mathbf k)
    \;=\; (ik_j)\!\left(\frac{ik_i}{k^2}\right)\hat\delta(\mathbf k)
    \;=\; -\frac{k_ik_j}{k^2}\,\hat\delta(\mathbf k),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ each Fourier mode of $\delta$ is mapped into a deformation-tensor
mode by the prefactor $-k_ik_j/k^2$. Writing $\hat{\mathbf k}=\mathbf k/k$
this prefactor is just $-\hat k_i \hat k_j$, which depends only on the
\emph{direction} of $\mathbf k$, not on $|\mathbf k|$. Its magnitude
is bounded by unity (Cauchy--Schwarz: $|k_i|, |k_j| \le k$, so
$|k_ik_j|\le k^2$).

Unlike $\nabla\delta$ (kernel $ik$, weighted by $|\mathbf k|$ and so
dominated by small scales) or $\boldsymbol\Psi^{(1)}$ (kernel
$i\mathbf k/k^2$, weighted by $1/|\mathbf k|$ and so dominated by
large scales), the deformation tensor has no preferred scale. Each
Fourier mode of $\delta$ contributes to $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ with at
most unit amplitude, regardless of $|\mathbf k|$. \textbf{Restriction
errors near the coarse Nyquist therefore enter $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ at
their full amplitude, without the $1/|\mathbf k|$ damping that
protects $\boldsymbol\Psi^{(1)}$.}

This error propagates into $S^{(2)}$ at second order. Each product
$\Psi^{(1)}_{i,i}\Psi^{(1)}_{j,j}$ or $(\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j})^2$ in
(\ref{eq:s2}) has a fractional error of approximately $2\epsilon$ when
the input deformation tensor has fractional error $\epsilon$. Where
the two products in (\ref{eq:s2}) nearly cancel, the relative error in
$S^{(2)}$ can be larger still. \textbf{As a result, $S^{(2)}$ is the LPT object
most sensitive to restriction.}

Figure~\ref{fig:deformation} confirms this hierarchy. The top row
shows $\Psi^{(1)}_{x,x}$ computed by (a) solving for $\Psi^{(1)}$ on
the fine grid and restricting to the coarse grid, and (b) restricting
$\delta$ first and then solving on the coarse grid. The two agree
closely; the difference is small and localised to regions of fine
structure, and the histogram of the per-cell relative difference has
an RMS of a few percent. The bottom row shows $S^{(2)}$ built from
the same two inputs. The disagreement is visibly larger and the
histogram is correspondingly broader, as expected from the
error-doubling argument above.

\section{Restricting the displacement potential is Poisson-inconsistent}\label{sec:phi}

An apparent shortcut is to solve the Poisson equation once on the
fine grid for $\phi^{(1)}_{2N}$, restrict $\phi^{(1)}_{2N}\to\phi^{(1)}_N$,
and then take the gradient on the coarse grid. This is not equivalent
to restricting $\delta$ and then solving Poisson on the coarse grid.
The table below summarises three coarse-grid constructions of $\Psi_x^{(1)}$.
Writing the contribution of a fine-grid mode $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k_c +
2\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_c$ to the coarse mode $\mathbf k_c$ as a
prefactor multiplying $\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k')$:
\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
construction & principal ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$) & alias ($\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$) \\
\hline
(1) fine $\Psi$, then restrict\quad ($\R\,\Psi^{(1)}_x[\delta_{2N}]$)
   & $i k_{c,x}\,W(\mathbf k_c)/k_c^{\,2}$
   & $i k'_x\,W(\mathbf k')/(k')^{2}$ \\
(2) restrict $\delta$, then solve\quad ($\Psi^{(1)}_x[\R\,\delta_{2N}]$)
   & $i k_{c,x}\,W(\mathbf k_c)/k_c^{\,2}$
   & $i k_{c,x}\,W(\mathbf k')/k_c^{\,2}$ \\
(3) restrict $\phi^{(1)}$, then $-\nabla\phi^{(1)}$\quad ($-\partial_x(\R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N})$)
   & $i k_{c,x}\,W(\mathbf k_c)/k_c^{\,2}$
   & $i k_{c,x}\,W(\mathbf k')/(k')^{2}$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Three coarse-grid constructions of $\Psi_x^{(1)}$, written as
prefactors of $\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k')$ for the principal coarse
mode ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$, i.e.\ $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k_c$) and for
its aliases ($\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k_c+2\pi\mathbf
n/\Delta x_c$). All three agree on the principal mode; the alias
prefactors differ.}
\label{tab:constructions}
\end{table}
The principal ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$) prefactor is identical for all
three constructions, so they agree on the resolved coarse modes. The
alias prefactors differ. Construction~(2) replaces the full
fine-mode kernel $i\mathbf k'/(k')^{2}$ (magnitude $1/k'$) by the
coarse-mode kernel $i\mathbf k_c/k_c^{\,2}$ (magnitude $1/k_c$), which
amplifies aliased high-$k$ power as discussed in \S\ref{sec:psi}.
Construction~(3) keeps the $1/(k')^{2}$ suppression of the inverse
Laplacian (only the gradient numerator changes from $ik'_x$ to
$ik_{c,x}$), so its alias amplitude tracks construction~(1) more
closely than construction~(2) does.

Amplitude alone, however, is not the correct criterion. The lo-res
1LPT displacement should be the one that solves Poisson on the coarse
grid sourced by the lo-res density. Construction~(3) does not satisfy
this relation: the restricted potential
$\phi^{(1)}_N \equiv \R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N}$
does not solve the coarse-grid Poisson equation
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2 \phi^{(1)}_N \;=\; \delta_N
\end{equation}
sourced by $\delta_N$. Define the implied effective density
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\rm eff}(\mathbf q) \;\equiv\; \nabla^2\phi^{(1)}_N(\mathbf q).
\end{equation}
Then $\delta_{\rm eff}\ne\delta_N$, and $-\nabla\phi^{(1)}_N$ is the
1LPT displacement of $\delta_{\rm eff}$ rather than of $\delta_N$.

Figure~\ref{fig:phi} illustrates this. The top row shows the three
$\Psi_x^{(1)}$ maps, which agree visually as expected from the table.
The bottom-left and bottom-middle panels show the errors of
constructions~(2) and~(3) against the fine reference. The bottom-right panel shows
$\delta_{\rm eff}-\delta_N$: it is non-zero and traces the underlying
density, confirming that $\phi^{(1)}_N$ does not encode the same source
as $\delta_N$.

\paragraph{Practical consequences.}
Path~(3)'s inconsistency has different implications for unigrid and
zoom-in runs. \emph{Unigrid runs:} once the simulation begins, the
gravitational potential is computed from the actual particle
distribution, which is the displacement field of $\delta_{\rm eff}$,
not of $\delta_N$. Statistical observables --- $P(k)$, halo mass
function, $\xi(r)$ --- are biased at the level of Fig.~\ref{fig:phi-spectral}
($\sim 5\%$ near the Nyquist for $n=\toyn$), with the largest effects
on the smallest resolved haloes. The BAO scale and other large-scale
signatures live at low $k$, where principal modes dominate and
$\hat\delta_{\rm eff}(\mathbf k_c)\approx\hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c)$
mode-by-mode (the two agree exactly on the principal $\mathbf n=0$
term and differ only by the alias contributions, which are negligible
at low $k_c$), so they are unaffected. The errors
are typically cosmetic for cosmology but visible at the sub-percent
precision targeted by modern emulators. \emph{Zoom-in runs:} \textbf{the
inconsistency lives in the lo-res region surrounding the zoom and
introduces a step in $\nabla\Phi$ at the coarse-fine interface,
because particles inside the zoom are displaced by $\delta_{2N}$ and
those outside by $\delta_{\rm eff}$.} The discontinuity heats gas near
the boundary (in hydro), tidally torques nearby haloes, and distorts
the inner density profiles of haloes within a few coarse cells of the
interface; substructure abundance in the affected zone is also wrong.
\citet{Hahn2011} quantified this for the older Fourier-truncation
\texttt{GRAFIC2} method, finding velocity errors that oscillate across
the entire refinement region with two orders of magnitude larger
amplitude than \texttt{MUSIC}'s, with $\sim 1\%$ shifts in bulk halo
properties and several-percent shifts in profiles. The modern
production codes (\texttt{MUSIC2}, \texttt{monofonIC}) avoid this
mode entirely by using the white-noise $k$-truncation strategy
(approach B in \S\ref{sec:zoom}).

\section{Comparison with a CDM spectrum}\label{sec:cdm}

The toy $P(k)\propto k^n$ used above (with $n=\toyn$ in our figures) is
chosen as the 2D analog of the high-$k$ behavior of the physical
$\Lambda$CDM (cold dark matter with cosmological constant) matter
power spectrum. In 3D, $\Lambda$CDM at high $k$ (well above the
matter-radiation equality wavenumber
$k_{\rm eq}\sim 0.01\,h\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$) behaves as
$P_{3D}(k)\sim k^{n_s-4}\sim k^{-3}$, where $n_s\approx 0.965$ is the
primordial spectral tilt (with a logarithmic correction from the
radiation-era transfer function).

The literal slope $n=-3$ is a 3D quantity and does not translate
directly to a 2D demonstration. The relevant invariant for restriction
effects is the variance contribution per logarithmic $k$ bin:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\sigma^2}{d\ln k} \;\propto\;
  \begin{cases}
    k^{3}\,P_{3D}(k) & \text{(3D)},\\[2pt]
    k^{2}\,P_{2D}(k) & \text{(2D)}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For 3D $\Lambda$CDM at high $k$, $k^3 P_{3D}\sim k^0$ --- a constant
contribution per log bin (so the variance integral diverges only
logarithmically). Imposing the same flat-per-log-bin behavior in 2D
requires $k^2 P_{2D}\sim k^0$, i.e.\ $P_{2D}\propto k^{-2}$. This is
the 2D power spectrum whose window and alias effects under restriction
behave qualitatively like those of 3D $\Lambda$CDM, which is why we
take $n=\toyn$ in the figures.

The toy still misses the low-$k$ turnover at $k_{\rm eq}$ where the
$\Lambda$CDM spectrum bends from $P(k)\sim k$ (large scales) to
$P(k)\sim k^{-3}$ (small scales). To check whether the conclusions
change qualitatively when the input includes that turnover,
Figure~\ref{fig:cdm} repeats the comparisons using a $\Lambda$CDM
matter power spectrum at $z=200$, generated with \texttt{CLASS} (a
public Boltzmann code that integrates the linear cosmological
perturbation equations through the radiation, matter, and dark-energy
eras), in a true 3D box with $L=500\,h^{-1}$~Mpc, $2N=128$, $N=64$,
and $2\times 2\times 2$ block-averaging restriction.

For each of $\Psi_x^{(1)}$ (top row) and $S^{(2)}$ (bottom row), the
figure compares the three coarse-grid constructions of \S\ref{sec:phi}:
construction (1) (fine $\Psi$/$S^{(2)}$ then restrict; the reference),
construction (2) (restrict $\delta$ then solve), and construction (3)
(restrict $\phi^{(1)}$ then differentiate). The leftmost panel shows a
$z$-slice of the reference (1), normalised by its own RMS; the middle
two panels show the residual $z$-slices $(1)-(2)$ and $(1)-(3)$ on the
same normalisation; the rightmost panel histograms the per-cell
relative error of (2) and (3) versus (1) over the full 3D volume.

The numbers confirm the alias-prefactor ordering of
Table~\ref{tab:constructions}. For $\Psi_x^{(1)}$, the relative RMS is
$\sim 17\%$ for $(1)-(2)$ and $\sim 12\%$ for $(1)-(3)$: construction
(3) is visibly closer to (1) than (2) is, because (3) keeps the
$1/(k')^2$ suppression of the inverse Laplacian and only changes the
gradient numerator, while (2) changes the full kernel from $1/k'$ to
$1/k_c$ and amplifies aliases by the largest factor. For $S^{(2)}$,
the relative RMS is $\sim 48\%$ for both $(1)-(2)$ and $(1)-(3)$, with
per-cell deviations exceeding $\sigma_{S^{(2)}}$ in either: the
squaring of deformation-tensor errors at second order, combined with
the lack of $1/|\mathbf k|$ damping in the kernel $-k_ik_j/k^2$, makes
$S^{(2)}$ the LPT object most sensitive to restriction. Two effects also amplify the residuals relative to what the toy
showed. First, in 3D each coarse mode has
seven alias partners (one per non-principal octant of the fine zone)
rather than three in 2D, so the alias sum has more terms.

Second, the denominator $\sigma_{\Psi^{(1)}}$ that we normalise the
residual by is itself much smaller for CDM than for the toy. The
variance per logarithmic $k$ bin of $\Psi^{(1)}$ is, up to a constant,
\[
  \frac{d\sigma^2_{\Psi^{(1)}}}{d\ln k} \;\propto\;
  \begin{cases}
    P(k)            & \text{(2D)},\\[2pt]
    k\,P(k)         & \text{(3D)},
  \end{cases}
\]
so the field's variance is concentrated wherever this combination
peaks. For the 2D toy $P(k)\propto k^{-2}$, $d\sigma^2/d\ln k\propto
k^{-2}$ keeps growing toward small $k$, and $\sigma^2_{\Psi^{(1)}}$ is
dominated by a few of the lowest box modes (those near $k_{\min}=2\pi/L$).
That single huge IR contribution puffs up the denominator while the
residual --- which lives at the high-$k$ end near the coarse Nyquist
--- is unchanged, so the relative error looks small. For 3D CDM, on
the other hand, $kP(k)$ peaks at $k_{\rm eq}\sim 0.01\,h\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$
and falls on either side; the variance is contributed by a broad
range of intermediate scales, no single low-$k$ mode dominates, and
the denominator is finite and modest. The residual at $k_{\mathrm{Ny},c}$
is then a larger fraction of it.

\section{Application: zoom-in IC generation}\label{sec:zoom}

The bin-averaging analysis of \S\ref{sec:fourier} and the Poisson
inconsistency of \S\ref{sec:phi} apply directly to the construction of
\emph{zoom-in} initial conditions, in which a fine-resolution region
(the zoom) sits inside a larger lo-res box. The lo-res region must be
constructed in a way that is consistent with the hi-res zoom interior
at their shared scales.

We compare three constructions. The first two start by generating the
full density (or potential) on the fine grid and building the lo-res
region outside the zoom by block averaging:
\begin{description}
\item[A1: bin-average $\delta$.]
  Set $\delta_{\rm lo} = \R\,\delta_{2N}$ outside the zoom, then solve
  Poisson on the coarse grid sourced by $\delta_{\rm lo}$ to get
  $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rm lo}$.
\item[A2: bin-average $\phi^{(1)}$.]
  Compute $\phi^{(1)}_{2N}$ on the fine grid, set
  $\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo} = \R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N}$, and take the gradient on
  the coarse grid:
  $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rm lo} = -\nabla\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo}$.
\end{description}
Both have problems, which we analyse below. The third is the standard
construction used in production codes:
\begin{description}
\item[B: $k$-truncate $\mu$.]
  Take the same fine-grid white noise $\mu$, set its modes above the
  coarse Nyquist to zero to obtain $\mu_{\rm trunc}$, and convolve
  with the transfer function:
  $\delta_{\rm lo} = \mathcal F^{-1}[T(k)\,\hat\mu_{\rm trunc}(\mathbf k)]$.
  Solve Poisson on the coarse grid sourced by $\delta_{\rm lo}$.
\end{description}
Construction~B is consistent at all coarse-grid scales.

\paragraph{Why A1 fails (cell-window deficit).}
The alias-sum formula~(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}) gives
\begin{equation}
  \R\,\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c) \;=\; W(\mathbf k_c)\,\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)
  \;+\; \sum_{\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0} W(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\,\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f).
\end{equation}
The principal $W(\mathbf k_c)\,\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)$ term is
suppressed by $W = \cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$ along each axis, dropping
to $\cos(\pi/4)\approx 0.71$ at the coarse Nyquist along an axis and
to $\cos^2(\pi/4)=1/2$ at the 2D corner. The alias terms
($\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$) add fine-grid power at $|\mathbf k'| >
k_{\mathrm{Ny},N}$, weighted by $W(\mathbf k')$. \textbf{For a red spectrum
($n=\toyn$) those aliased contributions are small because both $W$
and $\delta_{2N}$ are small at high $k'$; the mismatch with the
hi-res zoom interior is then dominated by the principal-mode deficit.}
For bluer spectra (or white noise) the aliased power is comparable to
the principal and the boundary mismatch acquires both
amplitude-suppression and alias-folded contributions. In either case,
inside the zoom region the field carries all fine modes; outside it
carries only the alias-summed coarse-grid version. \textbf{The near-Nyquist
amplitude mismatch (modes that both grids resolve, but the lo-res copy
is suppressed by $W$) is a \emph{boundary discontinuity in $\delta$}
at the zoom edge.}

The lo-res displacement $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rm lo}$ is internally
Poisson-consistent with the lo-res $\delta_{\rm lo}$ (we solved Poisson
on the coarse grid sourced by it), so there is no inconsistency
\emph{within} the lo-res region. The failure is the boundary mismatch
with the hi-res zoom interior.

\paragraph{Why A2 fails (Poisson inconsistency).}
The Poisson-inconsistency result of \S\ref{sec:phi} says
$\nabla^2(\R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N}) \neq \R\,\delta_{2N}$. The displacement
$\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rm lo} = -\nabla(\R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N})$ is the
1LPT displacement of an effective density
$\delta_{\rm eff} \equiv \nabla^2(\R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N})$, which equals
neither the bin-averaged density nor the original $\delta_{2N}$.

To compare $\delta_{\rm eff}$ with $\delta_N\equiv\R\,\delta_{2N}$
mode by mode, walk through the Fourier-space chain. Writing
$\mathbf k' = \mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ for the fine-grid
modes that alias into a coarse mode $\mathbf k_c$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Solve Poisson on the fine grid:
      $\hat\phi_{2N}(\mathbf k') = -\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k')/(k')^2$
      (the high-$k$ damping of the displacement potential).
\item Restrict $\phi$:
      $\widehat{\R\,\phi_{2N}}(\mathbf k_c) =
      \sum_{\mathbf n} W(\mathbf k')\,\hat\phi_{2N}(\mathbf k')$
      (the alias-sum formula applied to $\phi$).
\item Take the coarse Laplacian:
      $\hat\delta_{\rm eff}(\mathbf k_c)
      = -k_c^{\,2}\,\widehat{\R\,\phi_{2N}}(\mathbf k_c)
      = \sum_{\mathbf n} W(\mathbf k')\,\dfrac{k_c^{\,2}}{(k')^{2}}\,
        \hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k')$.
\end{enumerate}
Compare with the bin-averaged density,
$\hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c) = \sum_{\mathbf n}
W(\mathbf k')\,\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k')$. The two agree exactly on
the principal mode ($\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k_c$),
where the prefactor $k_c^{\,2}/(k')^{2}=1$. They differ on the
aliases ($\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$, $|\mathbf k'|>k_{\mathrm{Ny},N}$):
$\delta_{\rm eff}$'s alias contributions carry an extra factor
$k_c^{\,2}/(k')^{2}<1$, because the Poisson solve damped each fine
mode by $1/(k')^2$ but the coarse Laplacian only put back $k_c^{\,2}$
(not $(k')^2$, which the coarse grid cannot represent). Net effect:
$\delta_{\rm eff}$ is the bin-averaged density with its aliases
weakened.

\textbf{So at the boundary the cell-window deficit is similar to that of A1.
The more important problem is that $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rm lo}$ and
$\delta_{\rm lo}$ refer to different underlying fields: inside the
zoom, particles are displaced according to $\delta_{2N}$; outside,
they are displaced according to $\delta_{\rm eff}$. The two regions
therefore correspond to different density distributions.}

\paragraph{Why B is consistent (flat noise spectrum).}
For approach B, the lo-res field is
$\delta_{\rm lo}(\mathbf k_c) = T(\mathbf k_c)\,\hat\mu(\mathbf k_c)$
for $|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_c$ and zero otherwise. There is no cell-window
suppression and no aliasing, because $\mu$ is band-limited
\emph{before} the convolution with $T(k)$. ``Band-limited'' here means
$\hat\mu_{\rm trunc}(\mathbf k)$ is set to zero outside the coarse
Brillouin zone --- i.e.\ for any axis with $|k_a| > \pi/\Delta x_c$ ---
before $T(k)$ is applied. Multiplying by $T(k)$ does not introduce new
modes, so $T(\mathbf k)\hat\mu_{\rm trunc}(\mathbf k)$ is also zero
outside the coarse Brillouin zone. With no out-of-zone modes to fold
back, sub-sampling to the coarse grid produces no alias sum --- in
contrast to A1/A2, where $\delta_{2N}$ (or $\phi_{2N}$) carries
fine-grid power up to the fine Brillouin zone
($|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_f$) and the block-average $\R$ folds the modes
between the two zones into the coarse modes.

This works because white noise has a flat power spectrum:
$P_\mu(\mathbf k)=P_0$, independent of $\mathbf k$. Substituting
$P_{2N}=P_0$ into the alias-sum formula~(\ref{eq:Pk-alias}) applied
to $\mu$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pk-alias-white}
  P_N^\mu(\mathbf k_c) \;=\; P_0\sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^d}
        \bigl|W(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\bigr|^2.
\end{equation}
The kernel $W$ in eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-alias}) stands for whichever
restriction is in use; write $W_{\rm B}$ for the kernel of approach~B
and $W_{\rm A1}$ for the block-average kernel of approach~A1. In
approach~B the restriction is sharp truncation: a fine mode is kept
unchanged if it sits inside the coarse Brillouin zone (every axis
$|k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_c$) and is discarded otherwise, so
\begin{equation*}
  W_{\rm B}(\mathbf k) \;=\; \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } |k_a|\le\pi/\Delta x_c \text{ on every axis,}\\
    0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
The A1 kernel derived in eq.~(\ref{eq:Wcell}),
$W_{\rm A1}(\mathbf k)=\prod_a\cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$, is by contrast
nonzero everywhere up to the fine Brillouin zone --- which is what
lets the $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ alias terms contribute. For
$W_{\rm B}$ each $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ shift moves at least one
component of $\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f$ outside the coarse
zone, so only the $\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$ term in
eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-alias-white}) survives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pk-alias-white-truncated}
  P_N^{\mu_{\rm trunc}}(\mathbf k_c) \;=\; P_0 .
\end{equation}
The truncated white noise is still white, on a coarser grid. The
aliased contributions $\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0$ that A1 would have added
on top of the principal term carry the same flat amplitude $P_0$, so
dropping them does not bias the statistics: it removes equal-amplitude
copies of an isotropic spectrum. Convolving with $T(k)$ then produces
a coarse-grid density field that matches a fully resolved hi-res field
for all $|\mathbf k|\le k_{\rm Ny,N}$.

In production codes such as \texttt{MUSIC2} v2, the same property is
realised by generating white noise independently on each refinement
level and combining the levels in Fourier space: each level supplies
the modes between its parent's Nyquist and its own. Approach~B is the
simplest version of this idea. See \notecosmoic for
the \texttt{MUSIC2} pipeline in detail, and
\noteicsampling for a broader discussion of how white
noise is generated in IC codes.

\paragraph{Spectral diagnostics.}
Figure~\ref{fig:zoom-ic-pk} repeats the comparison in terms of binned
power spectra. The top row shows the lo-res $P(k)$ of each
construction and its ratio to the input theory. A1 and A2 both develop
a deficit that grows toward the coarse Nyquist
($|\mathbf k|/k_{\rm Ny,N}\to 1$); B agrees with the theory across the
full coarse-grid range. We use the fix-and-pair construction
\citep{Angulo2016}, a variance-reduction technique for cosmological
ICs (see \texttt{cosmo\_ic.tex} \S\,Reduced-variance sampling for a
full derivation). \emph{Fixing}: each Fourier mode of the white noise
$\hat\mu_{\mathbf k}$ has its (random) amplitude replaced by the
deterministic value $|\hat\mu_{\mathbf k}| = \sqrt{N_{\rm tot}}$,
where $N_{\rm tot}$ is the total number of grid points, while the
phase is left random and uniform on $[0,2\pi)$; so the convolution with
$T(k)$ produces $|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2 = P(k)\,V$ mode-by-mode
rather than only in the ensemble mean. \emph{Pairing}: a second
realisation is generated with all phases shifted by $\pi$ (i.e.\
$\hat\mu_{\mathbf k}\!\to\!-\hat\mu_{\mathbf k}$), so the leading
non-Gaussian contributions to two-point statistics cancel when the
pair is averaged. For the per-mode $P(k)$ check in
Fig.~\ref{fig:zoom-ic-pk} the fix alone is what matters: it removes
the per-mode cosmic-variance scatter and isolates the systematic
effect of restriction.

The bottom row uses the same setup to check the standard particle-based
IC validation procedure. Particles are placed at the fine-grid
Lagrangian positions, displaced by 2LPT, and deposited on the fine
grid using 2D cloud-in-cell (CIC) interpolation. The raw measured
$P(k)$ is suppressed by the CIC window
$W_{\rm CIC}(\mathbf k) = \prod_a\mathrm{sinc}(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$
(squared in the power spectrum); deconvolving by $W_{\rm CIC}^2$
recovers the input theory for all $|\mathbf k|<k_{\rm Ny,2N}$. The two
rows are on different grids (top: coarse; bottom: fine) and address
different questions: restriction error in the lo-res construction
versus window correction in the particle pipeline. We show them
together because they share the same setup.

\paragraph{Remaining limitation of approach B.}
Even with construction~B, the high-$k$ modes ($|\mathbf k|>k_{\rm Ny,N}$)
carried by the hi-res zoom interior cannot be represented on the lo-res
grid outside the zoom. This is a Nyquist limit, not an algorithmic
defect. Production zoom-IC codes address it in two complementary ways:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Surround the zoom region with a \emph{buffer zone} of additional
      hi-res cells. The boundary error decays away from the interface
      on a scale of $\sim 1/k_{\rm Ny,N}$ (one coarse cell), so the
      science region --- which lies further inside the buffer --- is
      not affected.
\item Use a multigrid (or hybrid real-space multigrid plus FFT)
      Poisson solver that couples all refinement levels in a single
      self-consistent solve, rather than running an independent
      FFT-Poisson on each level. This removes spurious mismatches in
      the gravitational potential at the level boundaries, which would
      otherwise appear if each level were treated as periodic in
      isolation.
\end{enumerate}
Both are layered on top of the basic requirement that the lo-res region
be constructed by $k$-truncation (B) rather than by bin-averaging
$\delta$ or $\phi^{(1)}$ (A1, A2).

\section{Validating zoom-in ICs}\label{sec:zoom-validation}

Section~\ref{sec:zoom} compared three constructions for the lo-res
region of a zoom IC. In production, the practical question is: given a
candidate construction, how do we test that it is correct
\emph{before} running an expensive $N$-body simulation? This section
presents two field-level diagnostics that probe the failure modes of
\S\S\ref{sec:fourier} and \ref{sec:phi}, together with the
re-simulation test used as the final check on the full pipeline.

A first guess --- estimating the global $P(k)$ of the assembled IC ---
has structural difficulties, which we discuss before introducing the
diagnostics.

\subsection{Why a global $P(k)$ of the assembled IC is the wrong tool}\label{sec:val-why-not-pk}

The assembled zoom IC has fine-resolution information inside the zoom
region $\Omega$ and coarse-resolution information outside. Producing a
single $P(k)$ that summarises both requires either upsampling the lo-res
field to the fine grid (which cannot recover the missing high-$k$ modes)
or applying an FFT to a global grid that is treated as periodic.
Neither approach is fully consistent.

The hi-res structure inside $\Omega$ does not continue smoothly into
the lo-res region outside, so the assembled $\delta_{\rm zoom}(\mathbf x)$
effectively carries a window function in real space. To model this:
introduce an apodisation window $W(\mathbf x)$ that tapers from $1$
in the deep interior of (say) the lo-res region to $0$ at the
boundary, and form $\delta_W(\mathbf x) = W(\mathbf x)\,\delta(\mathbf x)$.
The convolution theorem (\S\ref{app:convthm}) gives
$\hat\delta_W = \hat W \star \hat\delta$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:windowed-pk}
  \langle |\hat\delta_W(\mathbf k)|^2 \rangle
  \;=\; \int \frac{d^d q}{(2\pi)^d}\,
        |\hat W(\mathbf k-\mathbf q)|^2\, P(\mathbf q),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the true $P$ convolved with the window's power spectrum. When
$|\hat W|^2$ is narrow compared to features in $P$, pull $P$ out of
the integral and use Parseval to evaluate the remaining integral as
$\int W^2 d^d x$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Veff-norm}
  \langle |\hat\delta_W(\mathbf k)|^2 \rangle
  \;\approx\; P(\mathbf k)\,V_{\rm eff},
  \qquad V_{\rm eff} \equiv \int W^2(\mathbf x)\,d^d x.
\end{equation}
Equation~(\ref{eq:Veff-norm}) is the normalisation behind the
\citet{FKP1994} $P(k)$ estimator for masked galaxy surveys: there the
window $W(\mathbf x)$ is the survey selection function
$\bar n(\mathbf x)\,w(\mathbf x)$, the field of interest is observed
only inside the survey footprint, and the estimator divides the
periodogram of the masked field by $V_{\rm eff}=\int W^2\,d^d x$ to
recover $P(k)$. The bias from the convolution with $|\hat W|^2$ is
tolerable because $P(k)$ is unknown to begin with and is reported in
broad $k$-bins; the lowest-$k$ bins are either dropped or compared to
theory after explicitly convolving the theory $P(k)$ with $|\hat W|^2$. For \emph{validating} an IC
the target is the input linear $P(k)$ that was fed to MUSIC2 in the
first place (the CLASS/CAMB transfer function evaluated at
$z_{\rm start}$): the field $\delta(\mathbf x)$ is supposed to be a
Gaussian realisation with exactly that power spectrum, and the
question is whether it is. In this setting eq.~(\ref{eq:Veff-norm})
has two limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Modes with $k\lesssim 2\pi/L_\Omega$ are not resolved by the
      window, and modes within a couple of bins of that limit are
      biased by the window's $k$-width. The lowest-$k$ bins are
      contaminated.
\item It is power-only. If a construction produces the right
      \emph{amplitude} $|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2$ but the wrong
      Fourier coefficients $\hat\delta(\mathbf k)$, it still passes.
      Approach A2 fails in just this way: $\delta_{\rm eff}$ has
      roughly the right amplitude but is the wrong field.
\end{enumerate}
We therefore treat any global windowed $P(k)$ check as an approximate
sanity test rather than as a primary diagnostic.

\subsection{Per-level $P(k)$ on rectangular sub-grids}\label{sec:val-perlevel}

A cleaner spectral diagnostic uses the fact that each grid level is
internally periodic. The lo-res field $\delta_{\rm lo}$ is defined on
the coarse grid covering the full box; we apply the FFT directly and
compare to the input theory $P(k)$ for $|\mathbf k|\le k_{\rm Ny,N}$.
The hi-res field $\delta_{\rm hi}$ is defined on the fine grid covering
$\Omega$, treated as a periodic sub-box; we apply the FFT directly and
compare to $P(k)$ for $2\pi/L_\Omega\le|\mathbf k|\le k_{\rm Ny,2N}$.
Where the two $k$ ranges overlap, the two measurements should agree.

This is what is plotted in the top row of Fig.~\ref{fig:zoom-ic-pk}:
each lo-res construction is FFT-ed on the coarse grid alone, no
apodisation. A1 and A2 develop a deficit that grows toward the coarse
Nyquist (the $W^2$ suppression of \S\ref{sec:fourier}); B sits at the
theory line. The figure uses fix-and-pair white noise to remove
cosmic-variance scatter and expose the systematic restriction effect
cleanly.

\subsection{Diagnostic 1: shared-white-noise field-level subtraction}\label{sec:val-fieldsub}

The sharpest test is a pointwise field comparison against a unigrid
reference at the fine resolution. The procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the zoom IC $\delta_{\rm zoom}(\mathbf x)$ via the
      construction under test.
\item Build a unigrid reference $\delta_{\rm ref}(\mathbf x)$ on the
      fine grid covering the full box, using the \emph{same
      realisation} of the white noise $\mu_{2N}$ that the zoom IC
      used.
\item Subtract: $\varepsilon(\mathbf x) \equiv
      \delta_{\rm zoom}(\mathbf x) - \delta_{\rm ref}(\mathbf x)$.
      Compare RMS in the deep interior of $\Omega$ (should be at the
      convolution-kernel truncation floor, typically a few times
      $10^{-5}\sigma$ to $10^{-4}\sigma$) against RMS at the coarse-fine
      boundary (carries the construction error of the lo-res method).
\end{enumerate}
Step~2 is essential: sharing $\mu_{2N}$ ensures that the unigrid
reference and the hi-res zoom interior agree mode by mode, not only in
statistics. The residual of the lo-res region against the reference
is then the deterministic field-level error of the construction
method, free of cosmic-variance noise.

The expected form of $\varepsilon$ outside $\Omega$ is method-specific.
For A1, the alias-sum formula~(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}) gives modewise
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps-A1}
  \hat\varepsilon_{\rm A1}(\mathbf k_c)
  \;=\; \bigl[W(\mathbf k_c)-1\bigr]\,\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c)
  \;+\; \sum_{\mathbf n\ne\mathbf 0}
        W(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\,
        \hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f),
\end{equation}
the $W{-}1$ deficit on the principal mode plus the alias terms. For
A2 the principal mismatch is identical; the alias terms are
extra-suppressed by $k_c^2/|\mathbf k_c+\pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f|^2$
(\S\ref{sec:phi}). For B,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps-B}
  \hat\varepsilon_{\rm B}(\mathbf k)
  \;=\; -\,T(\mathbf k)\,\hat\mu_{2N}(\mathbf k)\quad
  \text{for}\;|k_a|>\pi/\Delta x_c,\quad 0\;\text{otherwise};
\end{equation}
the lo-res grid cannot represent the truncated modes. This is the
irreducible boundary deficit discussed in \S\ref{sec:zoom}.

For real production codes, the expected scale of $\varepsilon$ in the
\emph{interior} of $\Omega$ is set by the convolution-kernel truncation
and by floating-point precision. \citet{Hahn2011} \S 4.3 / Fig.~15
report $\sigma(\varepsilon_\delta)/\sigma(\delta) \sim 7\times 10^{-5}$
within one refinement level (and $\sim 5\times 10^{-5}$ within two
nested levels), with errors confined to a one-cell-thick layer at the
coarse-fine interface. Velocity errors are
$\sim 1.5\times 10^{-3}\,\sigma_v$ overall and below
$10^{-4}\,\sigma_v$ in the interior. The older Fourier-based
\texttt{GRAFIC2} algorithm produces errors that oscillate across the
entire refinement region with amplitudes two orders of magnitude
larger (\citealt{Hahn2011}, Fig.~16). The property to verify is
therefore not the overall RMS alone, but that the RMS is dominated by
a thin boundary layer while the interior sits at the convolution-kernel
truncation floor ($\sim 10^{-5}$--$10^{-4}\,\sigma$ in the references
above), well below the boundary contribution but well above
floating-point precision.

\subsection{Diagnostic 2: Poisson-consistency residual}\label{sec:val-poisson}

The second diagnostic targets A2's failure mode specifically: that
the lo-res displacement $\boldsymbol\Psi_{\rm lo}$ and the lo-res
density $\delta_{\rm lo}$ may refer to inconsistent fields. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson-resid}
  r(\mathbf x) \;\equiv\; \nabla^2\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo}(\mathbf x)
                          - \delta_{\rm lo}(\mathbf x),
\end{equation}
computed on the coarse grid using the same Laplacian that the IC
code uses. By construction:
\begin{itemize}
\item In A1 and B, $\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo}$ is obtained by solving
      $\nabla^2\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo}=\delta_{\rm lo}$ on the coarse
      grid, so $r=0$ to floating-point precision.
\item In A2, $\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo} = \R\,\phi^{(1)}_{2N}$, while
      $\delta_{\rm lo}$ is whatever the user reports (typically
      $\R\,\delta_{2N}$). The result of \S\ref{sec:phi} guarantees
      $r\ne 0$, and the residual is structured (correlated with the
      underlying density), not noise.
\end{itemize}
Because A1 and A2 produce visually similar lo-res displacement maps
(both share the $W{-}1$ suppression on the principal mode), diagnostic~1
applied to the displacement alone may not distinguish A2 from A1.
Diagnostic~2 is the test that specifically detects the Poisson
inconsistency.

\subsection{Demonstration on the 2D toy}\label{sec:val-demo}

The two diagnostics are exactly the row-2 and row-3 panels of
Fig.~\ref{fig:zoom-ic-cmp}, restated as validation tests rather
than construction-method comparisons. Reading off the figure:
\begin{description}
\item[Diagnostic 1 (residual vs ideal lowpass).] A1 and A2 both
      show RMS $\sim 2\times 10^{-3}$, dominated by the $W{-}1$
      deficit on the principal mode; the alias and Poisson-inconsistency
      contributions are subdominant. B is exactly zero by
      construction (since the ideal-lowpass reference here is
      $\mathcal F^{-1}[T(k)\hat\mu_{\rm trunc}]$ sampled on the
      coarse grid, which is what B produces).
\item[Diagnostic 2 (Poisson-inconsistency residual).] A1 and B are
      at machine zero. A2 has RMS $\sim 5\times 10^{-4}$ with a
      structured spatial pattern. A2 fails this test by orders of
      magnitude relative to A1 and B.
\end{description}

The key point is the contrast between the two diagnostics: \emph{neither
catches both failure modes on its own}. A1 fails diagnostic~1
(cell-window deficit) but passes diagnostic~2, because it solves the
correct Poisson equation, only for the wrong density. A2 fails both;
in the displacement field alone it looks the same as A1, and only the
Poisson-residual map reveals its specific defect. B is the only
construction that passes both diagnostics. Any candidate zoom-IC
method should be checked against both tests.

\subsection{Re-simulation against a reference}\label{sec:val-resim}

The diagnostics above probe the IC at construction time. They cannot
detect secondary issues that appear only under non-linear evolution:
for example, \textbf{spurious force discontinuities at the coarse-fine
interface that gradually distort the inner density profile of objects
near the boundary, or correlations between IC errors and the late-time
abundance of the smallest haloes}. The standard whole-pipeline check
is a comparison against a controlled reference simulation. The
procedure and the list of compared quantities below follow
\citet{Hahn2011} \S 4.4 / Table 4; the algebraic remarks and the
connection to approach~A1 of \S\ref{sec:zoom} are not in Hahn and are
ours:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Generate a unigrid IC at the fine resolution covering the whole
      box, then build a ``degraded'' reference by averaging the
      unigrid field down to the lo-res resolution outside the zoom
      region. Concretely, this can be done in two equivalent ways
      (see the algebra below). Let $\mathbf q_c$ denote the Lagrangian
      position of a coarse cell (the geometric centre of its $2^d$
      fine sub-cells), and $\mathbf q_i$ for $i\in\text{block}(\mathbf q_c)$
      the Lagrangian positions of those fine sub-cells.
      (i) Block-average the fine displacement field
      $\boldsymbol\Psi_{2N}(\mathbf q)$ over each $2^d$-cell coarse
      block to get
      $\boldsymbol\Psi_N(\mathbf q_c)=\tfrac{1}{2^d}\sum_{i}\boldsymbol\Psi_{2N}(\mathbf q_i)$,
      then place one particle of mass $2^d m_{\rm fine}$ at
      $\mathbf q_c+\boldsymbol\Psi_N(\mathbf q_c)$;
      or (ii) take the $2^d$ fine particles in each block and replace
      them with a single particle at the centre-of-mass position with
      the mean velocity and total mass. Apply this only outside
      $\Omega$; inside $\Omega$ keep the original fine particles. The
      degraded IC has the same long-wavelength structure as the
      multi-scale zoom IC but skips the multi-scale construction
      machinery, and so serves as the ``what would unigrid have
      given'' baseline.

      \textit{Equivalence of (i) and (ii).} Fine particles in a
      $2^d$ block live at Eulerian positions
      $\mathbf x_i = \mathbf q_i + \boldsymbol\Psi(\mathbf q_i)$.
      Procedure~(ii) gives
      \begin{equation*}
        \mathbf x_{\rm COM} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2^d}\sum_i\mathbf x_i
        \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2^d}\sum_i\mathbf q_i}_{=\,\mathbf q_c}
        \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2^d}\sum_i\boldsymbol\Psi(\mathbf q_i)}_{=\,\langle\boldsymbol\Psi\rangle_{\rm block}} ,
      \end{equation*}
      where the first sum collapses because the $2^d$ fine Lagrangian
      positions are arranged symmetrically about the coarse Lagrangian
      centre $\mathbf q_c$. Procedure~(i) places the new particle at
      $\mathbf q_c+\boldsymbol\Psi_N(\mathbf q_c)
      =\mathbf q_c+\langle\boldsymbol\Psi\rangle_{\rm block}$,
      so $\mathbf x_{\rm COM}=\mathbf x_{\rm field}$ identically. The
      same argument applies to the velocity, which is a linear
      functional of the fine $\boldsymbol\Psi$.

      \textit{Relation to approach A1.} The restriction operator used
      here is exactly approach A1 of \S\ref{sec:zoom}: block-average
      $\boldsymbol\Psi_{2N}$ over $2^d$ fine cells, with Fourier
      multiplier $W_{\rm A1}(\mathbf k)=\prod_a\cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$.
      The same $\cos^2$ near-Nyquist suppression and alias-folding are
      therefore present in the degraded reference. What is \emph{not}
      present is the additional error that a real production zoom code
      makes at the coarse-fine boundary: real zoom codes do not have
      $\boldsymbol\Psi_{2N}$ outside $\Omega$ at all and generate the
      lo-res field there from a separate coarse white noise $\mu_N$,
      with the job of approaches A1/A2/B being to stitch the two
      regions together. In the degraded reference a single $\mu_{2N}$
      covers the whole box, so the long-wavelength modes inside and
      outside $\Omega$ come from the same underlying noise field and
      there is no boundary-stitching to get wrong. The degraded
      reference therefore inherits the suppression and aliasing of
      A1's $\cos^2$ Fourier multiplier --- the same $|W_{\rm A1}|^2$
      factor in eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-alias}) that the toy demo of
      \S\ref{sec:zoom} measured against ideal-lowpass~B --- but it
      does not inherit the boundary-stitching error, and is what defines ``correct'' for the
      comparison below: the zoom IC under test should reproduce the
      degraded reference's halo properties to within the cosmic-variance
      noise that survives non-linear evolution.
\item Run both ICs (multi-scale and degraded) forward to $z=0$ with
      the same $N$-body code and force softening.
\item Compare bulk halo properties: virial mass $M_{\rm vir}$,
      virial radius $R_{\rm vir}$, peak circular velocity $V_{\rm max}$
      and its radius $R_{\rm max}$, spin parameter $\lambda$, three
      dimensional velocity dispersion $\sigma_v$, minor-to-major axis
      ratio $c/a$. These are the cluster-level observables in
      Table~4 of \citet{Hahn2011}.
\item Compare radial density profiles $\rho(r)$ inside $R_{\rm vir}$
      and the substructure abundance $N(>V_{\rm max,sub})$. Profiles
      should agree at the few-percent level outside the innermost
      bin (which is sensitive to refinement-region size, not to the
      IC method).
\end{enumerate}
The published agreement between \texttt{MUSIC} and a degraded-unigrid
reference is at the $\sim 1\%$ level for bulk halo properties and at
the few-percent level for density profiles and substructure, with no
systematic bias. Reaching this level of agreement for a candidate
construction is strong evidence that the IC is correct across all
scales --- both those covered by the construction-time diagnostics of
\S\S\ref{sec:val-fieldsub}--\ref{sec:val-poisson} and those that are
not.

\section{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item Block-averaging restriction is a smoothing step (convolution with
      a small averaging kernel) followed by $2{:}1$ subsampling. In
      Fourier space the smoothing multiplies by the cell window
      $W(\mathbf k)=\prod_a\cos(k_a\Delta x_f/2)$, and the subsampling
      folds fine-grid power from beyond the coarse Nyquist wavenumber
      back onto the coarse modes (Eq.~\ref{eq:restrict-fourier},
      Fig.~\ref{fig:density}).
\item The 1LPT displacement $\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(1)}=i\mathbf k/k^2\,\hat\delta$
      is large-scale dominated and is therefore much more robust to
      restriction (Fig.~\ref{fig:psi}).
\item The deformation tensor $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ has kernel
      $-k_ik_j/k^2$ (bounded by unity) and shows a modest restriction
      error. The 2LPT source $S^{(2)}$, being quadratic in
      $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$, inherits a noticeably larger error
      (Fig.~\ref{fig:deformation}). This is the practically
      interesting cost of restriction in an LPT pipeline.
\item Restricting $\phi^{(1)}$ and then differentiating produces a
      $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ whose amplitude can be close to truth, but
      $\nabla^2\phi^{(1)}_N\ne\delta_N$. The result is not the 1LPT
      displacement of the restricted density (Fig.~\ref{fig:phi}).
      When working across multiple grid resolutions one should therefore
      restrict the source ($\delta$, or the residual of the Poisson
      equation in iterative solvers) and re-solve on the coarser grid,
      rather than restrict the potential itself.
\item Validating a candidate zoom-IC construction requires more than
      a global $P(k)$ check (which is window-contaminated and
      power-only). The two sharper diagnostics are (i) a shared-white-noise
      field-level subtraction $\varepsilon = \delta_{\rm zoom}-\delta_{\rm ref}$
      against a unigrid reference at the fine resolution, which catches
      A1's cell-window deficit (\S\ref{sec:val-fieldsub};
      \citet{Hahn2011} \S 4.3 reports interior RMS
      $\sim 10^{-4}\sigma_\delta$ for \texttt{MUSIC}); and (ii) a
      Poisson-consistency residual
      $|\nabla^2\phi^{(1)}_{\rm lo}-\delta_{\rm lo}|$, which catches
      A2's Poisson-inconsistency bug (\S\ref{sec:val-poisson}). Neither
      alone catches both failures; both together do (\S\ref{sec:val-demo},
      Fig.~\ref{fig:zoom-ic-cmp}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Convolution theorem in continuous and discrete settings}\label{app:convthm}

Several places in the main text use the fact that convolution in real
space becomes multiplication in Fourier space. This is the
\emph{convolution theorem}. The argument is essentially the same in
the continuous and discrete cases; we record both here.

\paragraph{Continuous case.}
Define the continuous Fourier transform with the convention
$\hat g(k) = \int g(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx$, and the convolution
\begin{equation}
  y(x) \;=\; (h*f)(x) \;\equiv\; \int h(x-x')\,f(x')\,dx'.
\end{equation}
Take the Fourier transform of $y$ and substitute the convolution
formula:
\begin{equation}
  \hat y(k) \;=\; \int y(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx
            \;=\; \iint h(x-x')\,f(x')\,e^{-ikx}\,dx\,dx'.
\end{equation}
Change variables $u = x-x'$ (so $x = u+x'$, $dx = du$), which makes
the exponent factor as $e^{-ikx} = e^{-iku}\,e^{-ikx'}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat y(k) \;=\; \iint h(u)\,f(x')\,e^{-iku}\,e^{-ikx'}\,du\,dx'
            \;=\; \Bigl(\int h(u)\,e^{-iku}\,du\Bigr)
                  \Bigl(\int f(x')\,e^{-ikx'}\,dx'\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The two factors are just $\hat h(k)$ and $\hat f(k)$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convthm-cont}
  \boxed{\;\hat y(k) \;=\; \hat h(k)\,\hat f(k).\;}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Discrete case.}
Now the inputs are sequences $h_n, f_n$ on a grid of spacing
$\Delta x$. Define the continuous-$k$ Fourier transform of a sequence
by $\hat g(k) = \sum_n g_n\,e^{-ikn\Delta x}$ (the same formula used
for $H(k)$ in the main text), and the discrete convolution
\begin{equation}
  y_m \;=\; (h*f)_m \;\equiv\; \sum_n h_n\,f_{m-n}.
\end{equation}
Take the Fourier transform of $y$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat y(k) \;=\; \sum_m y_m\,e^{-ikm\Delta x}
            \;=\; \sum_{m,n} h_n\,f_{m-n}\,e^{-ikm\Delta x}.
\end{equation}
Substitute $m' = m-n$ (so $m = m'+n$, exponent
$e^{-ikm\Delta x} = e^{-ikn\Delta x}\,e^{-ikm'\Delta x}$):
\begin{equation}
  \hat y(k) \;=\; \sum_{n,m'} h_n\,f_{m'}\,e^{-ikn\Delta x}\,e^{-ikm'\Delta x}
            \;=\; \Bigl(\sum_n h_n\,e^{-ikn\Delta x}\Bigr)
                  \Bigl(\sum_{m'} f_{m'}\,e^{-ikm'\Delta x}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
which gives the same product law,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convthm-disc}
  \boxed{\;\hat y(k) \;=\; \hat h(k)\,\hat f(k).\;}
\end{equation}
The substitution $m \to m'=m-n$ is the discrete analogue of the change
of variables $x \to u=x-x'$ in the continuous proof, and the rest of
the argument is identical: the double sum factors into a product of
single sums.

\paragraph{Convolution vs cross-correlation.}
The main text wrote the smoothing step as $y_m=\sum_n h_n\,f_{m+n}$
(a moving average), which is technically the discrete
\emph{cross-correlation} of $h$ with $f$, not the convolution. The two
differ by a sign-flip in $n$. For a kernel that is symmetric about its
support (the \emph{centered} version of our 2-sample kernel, with
weights $\tfrac12$ at $x=\pm\Delta x_f/2$, satisfies that property)
the two coincide, so there is no practical difference. The literal
sequence $h[0]=h[1]=\tfrac12$ used in the main text is not symmetric
about $n=0$, but its FT differs from the centered version's only by
the centroid-shift phase $e^{-ik\Delta x_f/2}$ discussed in
\S\ref{sec:fourier}, which drops out of any magnitude or
power-spectrum-level statement.

\section{Derivation of the alias-sum formula}\label{app:aliassum}

Equation~(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}) is the central Fourier-space
identity for restriction. Here we derive it by composing the two
restriction steps in real space and translating each into Fourier
space, tracking the normalization prefactor explicitly.

\paragraph{Convention.}
We use the unnormalized continuous-$k$ Fourier transform of a discrete
sequence (consistent with App.~A): for a sequence $g_n$ on a grid of
spacing $\Delta x$,
\begin{equation}
  \hat g(k) = \sum_n g_n\,e^{-ikn\Delta x}.
\end{equation}
This is the convention that makes the convolution
theorem~(\ref{eq:convthm-disc}) read as a clean product, and it is the
convention against which the prefactor below is computed.

\paragraph{Step 1 --- smoothing.}
The first step is convolution of the fine-grid samples with the
averaging kernel $h$. By the convolution theorem
(\ref{eq:convthm-disc}), this becomes multiplication in Fourier space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-smooth}
  \hat y(\mathbf k) \;=\; W(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k),
  \qquad W(\mathbf k) \equiv \hat h(\mathbf k).
\end{equation}
$\hat y$ still lives on the fine grid --- same wavenumbers as
$\hat\delta_{2N}$.

\paragraph{Step 2 --- subsampling.}
For a sequence $g_n$ on the fine grid (spacing $\Delta x_f$), the
subsampled sequence $g'_I=g_{2I}$ on the coarse grid (spacing
$\Delta x_c=2\Delta x_f$) has Fourier transform
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-subsample-1d}
  \hat g'(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\hat g(k) + \hat g(k + \pi/\Delta x_f)\Bigr]
  \qquad \text{(1D)}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Why this form.} The Fourier sum for $\hat g'(k)$ runs only
over even-index fine-grid samples. Define a multiplier $m_n$ that
equals $1$ for even $n$ and $0$ for odd $n$; then summing
$g_n\,m_n$ over all $n$ on the fine grid is the same as summing
$g_n$ over even $n$ only. The multiplier has the closed-form
expression
\begin{equation*}
  m_n = \tfrac12\bigl(1+e^{i\pi n}\bigr),
\end{equation*}
because $e^{i\pi n}=+1$ for even $n$ (giving $\tfrac12(1+1)=1$) and
$=-1$ for odd $n$ (giving $\tfrac12(1-1)=0$). Inserting this into
the Fourier sum,
\begin{align*}
  \hat g'(k)
  &= \sum_n g_n\,m_n\,e^{-ikn\Delta x_f} \\
  &= \tfrac12\sum_n g_n\,e^{-ikn\Delta x_f}
    \;+\;\tfrac12\sum_n g_n\,e^{-i(k-\pi/\Delta x_f)n\Delta x_f},
\end{align*}
where the second sum used $e^{i\pi n}=e^{i(\pi/\Delta x_f)n\Delta x_f}$ to
absorb the $e^{i\pi n}$ into a wavenumber shift. The two sums are
$\hat g(k)$ and $\hat g(k\pm\pi/\Delta x_f)$ respectively, giving
(\ref{eq:app-subsample-1d}). The shifted copy is the alias: it
encodes the high-frequency content $|k|>\pi/\Delta x_c$ of the
fine-grid sequence that subsampling cannot distinguish from the
in-zone partner.
Generalizing to $d$ dimensions with $2{:}1$ subsampling along each
axis, the prefactor becomes $1/2^d$ and the sum runs over $2^d$
shifts:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-subsample}
  \hat g'(\mathbf k) \;=\; \frac{1}{2^d}
  \sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^d}
  \hat g\!\left(\mathbf k + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f\right).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in real space subsampling is multiplication by a Dirac
comb of spacing $\Delta x_c$, which Fourier-transforms to another
Dirac comb of spacing $2\pi/\Delta x_c=\pi/\Delta x_f$, and convolving
$\hat g$ with that comb (over one fine-mode period) gives the same
sum.

\paragraph{Combine.}
Applying~(\ref{eq:app-subsample}) to $g=y$ and substituting
$\hat y = W\hat\delta_{2N}$ from~(\ref{eq:app-smooth}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-aliassum-rawconv}
  \hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c) \;=\; \frac{1}{2^d}
  \sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^d}
  W(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\,
  \hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f).
\end{equation}
The $\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$ term is the principal contribution
(multiplication by $W(\mathbf k_c)$ alone); the remaining $2^d-1$
terms (three in 2D, seven in 3D) are the alias contributions from
outer-wing fine modes that fold onto $\mathbf k_c$.

\paragraph{Reconciling with the main-text form.}
Equation~(\ref{eq:app-aliassum-rawconv}) is the alias-sum formula in
the unnormalized DTFT convention; it carries the prefactor $1/2^d$
(equivalently $1/M^d$ for $M{:}1$ subsampling). The main-text form
(\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}) drops this prefactor because the demo
scripts use the ``per-mode amplitude'' convention,
\begin{equation}
  \hat\delta_{2N}^{\rm pm}(\mathbf k) \equiv \hat\delta_{2N}(\mathbf k)/N_{2N}^d,
  \qquad
  \hat\delta_N^{\rm pm}(\mathbf k_c) \equiv \hat\delta_N(\mathbf k_c)/N_N^d,
\end{equation}
i.e., divide each grid's DFT by its total number of samples. Since
$N_{2N} = 2 N_N$, the relative normalization is $N_{2N}^d/N_N^d = 2^d$,
which exactly cancels the $1/2^d$ in
(\ref{eq:app-aliassum-rawconv}):
\begin{equation}
  \hat\delta_N^{\rm pm}(\mathbf k_c) \;=\;
  \sum_{\mathbf n\in\{0,1\}^d}
  W(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f)\,
  \hat\delta_{2N}^{\rm pm}(\mathbf k_c + \pi\mathbf n/\Delta x_f),
\end{equation}
which is (\ref{eq:restrict-fourier}). The choice of convention is a
matter of taste; the per-mode amplitude form is what NumPy's
\texttt{fftn} gives if you divide by the total grid size, and it is
the form most commonly used in cosmological power-spectrum estimation,
where one wants
$\langle|\hat\delta^{\rm pm}(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle = P(\mathbf k)/V$
to match the continuum normalization
$\langle\delta(\mathbf q_1)\delta(\mathbf q_2)\rangle = \int d^d k\,P(k)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf q_1-\mathbf q_2)}/(2\pi)^d$.

\section{Generating a Gaussian random field with prescribed $P(k)$}\label{app:grfgen}

The setup section uses the simplest of two equivalent ways to generate
a Gaussian random field with a target power spectrum $P(k)$ on a
periodic grid. Both produce the same statistical distribution; they
differ in code complexity and in how Hermitian symmetry is enforced.

\paragraph{Method A: white noise in real space, then FFT.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw a unit-variance white-noise field
      $\mu(\mathbf q)\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, independent and identically
      distributed (iid: same distribution at every site, statistically
      uncorrelated between sites) at every grid point.
\item Compute its Fourier transform $\hat\mu(\mathbf k)$.
\item Multiply mode-by-mode by $\sqrt{P(\mathbf k)}$ to colour the field:
      $\hat\delta(\mathbf k) = \sqrt{P(\mathbf k)}\,\hat\mu(\mathbf k)$.
\item Inverse-transform to real space; zero the
      $\mathbf k=\mathbf 0$ mode if you want exactly zero mean.
\end{enumerate}
Because $\mu$ is real, $\hat\mu(\mathbf k)$ is automatically
Hermitian-symmetric ($\hat\mu(-\mathbf k)=\hat\mu^*(\mathbf k)$) and
the inverse transform of $\hat\delta$ is therefore guaranteed real.
Special-case modes (DC and the Nyquist self-conjugate corners) are
real automatically.

\paragraph{Method B: white noise in Fourier space directly.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw a complex Gaussian Fourier coefficient at each independent
      mode:
      \begin{equation}
        \hat\delta(\mathbf k) = \sqrt{P(\mathbf k)/2}\,(u + iv),
        \qquad u,v\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\;\text{iid}.
      \end{equation}
      With this normalization,
      $\mathrm{Re}\,\hat\delta$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\hat\delta$ each have
      variance $P/2$, and $\langle|\hat\delta|^2\rangle = P$.
\item Equivalently in compact notation,
      $\hat\delta(\mathbf k) = \sqrt{P(\mathbf k)}\,z$ with
      $z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$, where the complex standard normal is
      $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)\equiv (u+iv)/\sqrt 2$ with $u,v\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ iid.
\item Enforce Hermitian symmetry by hand:
      $\hat\delta(-\mathbf k) = \hat\delta^*(\mathbf k)$.
\item For self-conjugate modes (DC; Nyquist corners in even-sized
      grids), draw a real Gaussian rather than a complex one (no
      imaginary part).
\item Inverse-transform to real space.
\end{enumerate}
Method B requires more bookkeeping but lets you set the seed of
individual modes (useful for ``fixed-and-paired'' simulations or for
zoom ICs) and saves one FFT.

\paragraph{Equivalence on the grid.}
On an infinite (or sufficiently large) box, the two methods produce
statistically identical fields with
$\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\rangle = P(\mathbf k)\,\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}$.
On a \emph{finite} periodic box, however, they differ in their
treatment of long-wavelength modes. Method~A produces a field whose
realised spectrum is the box-window-convolved $P(\mathbf k)\otimes|\tilde W_C(\mathbf k)|^2$ ---
each grid mode inherits contributions from neighbouring (and even
sub-fundamental) continuous-$k$ modes through the sinc envelope of the
box window. Method~B assigns each grid mode the input $P$ value
exactly, with no sub-fundamental power at all (and enforces
$P(\mathbf k=\mathbf 0)=0$). The discrepancy matters most for small
boxes where a non-negligible fraction of the variance lies near or
below $k_{\rm fund}=2\pi/L$; \citet{Sirko2005} traced a $\sigma_8$
bias in $P$-sampled small-box ICs to exactly this effect. See
\noteicsampling and references therein
\citep{Pen1997, Sirko2005, Hahn2011} for a thorough discussion of the
$\xi$-sampled (Method A) versus $P$-sampled (Method B) distinction
and the convolution picture proposed by \citet{Pen1997}. The choice between them is therefore not just code
simplicity --- it has a real effect on large scales for small boxes.
All figures in this note use Method~A; for the boxes we work in, the
distinction is small at the wavenumbers where restriction effects are
visible (well above $k_{\rm fund}$).

\section{Power spectrum: derivation of the two-point correlator}\label{app:pkdef}

This appendix derives eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-def}) and its discrete-box
counterpart from the definitions of homogeneity and the Fourier
transform. The aim is to make the volume factors and delta-function
conventions explicit so that the rest of the note can suppress them.

\subsection*{Continuous case}

Let $\delta(\mathbf x)$ be a real-valued, statistically homogeneous
random field on $\mathbb R^d$ with zero mean. \emph{Homogeneity} means
that the two-point correlation function depends only on the
separation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi-def}
  \xi(\mathbf r) \;\equiv\;
  \bigl\langle\delta(\mathbf x)\,\delta(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
with the right-hand side independent of $\mathbf x$. Here $\langle
\cdot\rangle$ denotes the \emph{ensemble average} over realizations
of the random field. If we further assume \emph{ergodicity} --- the property that
sufficiently distant regions of a single realization are
statistically independent and so behave like independent draws from
the ensemble --- the ensemble average equals a spatial average, so $\xi(\mathbf r)$ can be
estimated from a single sufficiently large realization by averaging
$\delta(\mathbf x)\delta(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)$ over $\mathbf x$.
This is the assumption that lets cosmologists measure $\xi(r)$ and
$P(k)$ from a single observed universe.
Define the Fourier transform with the symmetric ``physics''
convention,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ft-def}
  \hat\delta(\mathbf k) = \int d^dx\,\delta(\mathbf x)\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x},
  \qquad
  \delta(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,e^{+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}.
\end{equation}
Then the two-point correlator of Fourier modes is
\begin{align}
  \bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  &= \int d^dx\,d^dx'\,
     \bigl\langle\delta(\mathbf x)\,\delta(\mathbf x')\bigr\rangle
     e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'} \notag\\
  &= \int d^dx\,d^dx'\,\xi(\mathbf x'-\mathbf x)\,
     e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'},
\end{align}
using homogeneity in the second line. Change variables to $\mathbf
r=\mathbf x'-\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x$:
\begin{align}
  \bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  &= \int d^dx\,e^{-i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf x}
     \int d^dr\,\xi(\mathbf r)\,e^{i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf r} \notag\\
  &= (2\pi)^d\,\delta^D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\,P(\mathbf k'),
  \label{eq:Pk-derived}
\end{align}
where the $\mathbf x$ integral produced the Dirac delta
$\int d^dx\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}=(2\pi)^d\delta^D(\mathbf q)$,
and we identified
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-xi-FT}
  P(\mathbf k) \;\equiv\; \int d^dr\,\xi(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}
\end{equation}
as the Fourier transform of the correlation function (the
\emph{Wiener--Khinchin theorem}). On a periodic box of volume $V$
this is equivalent to the variance form $P(\mathbf
k)=\langle|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle/V$, derived in the
discrete-case subsection below. The Dirac delta is a distribution,
defined by its action under integration against a test function:
$\int d^dk'\,f(\mathbf k')\,\delta^D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k') = f(\mathbf
k)$. Applied to the right-hand side of (\ref{eq:Pk-derived}), it
forces $\mathbf k'\to\mathbf k$ wherever the expression is integrated
against a smooth function, so $P(\mathbf k')$ may be replaced by
$P(\mathbf k)$ without changing any observable. This is exactly
eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-def}).

A few remarks:
\begin{itemize}
\item Reality of $\delta(\mathbf x)$ implies $\hat\delta^*(\mathbf
k)=\hat\delta(-\mathbf k)$, so $P(\mathbf k)=P(-\mathbf k)$ and
$P$ is real and non-negative.
\item Statistical isotropy (invariance under rotations) further
reduces $\xi(\mathbf r)\to\xi(|\mathbf r|)$ and $P(\mathbf k)\to
P(|\mathbf k|)$.
\item Because $\delta^D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')$ vanishes for $\mathbf
k\ne\mathbf k'$, eq.~(\ref{eq:Pk-def}) says that distinct Fourier
modes have zero cross-correlation: each $\hat\delta(\mathbf k)$ is
its own independent random amplitude, with variance encoded in
$P(\mathbf k)$. This is the simplification homogeneity buys ---
the two-point function is non-trivial in real space but diagonal
in Fourier space.
\item The Dirac-delta form (\ref{eq:Pk-def}) is preferred as a
\emph{definition} because it is volume-independent --- a property
of the field itself, not of any box --- and it derives the
discrete form (\ref{eq:Pk-discrete}) by the standard substitution
$(2\pi)^d\delta^D(\mathbf q)\to V\delta_{\mathbf q,\mathbf 0}$.
The variance form $\langle|\hat\delta|^2\rangle/V$ is more
intuitive but requires a finite box to make sense.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Discrete case (periodic box)}

In numerical work the field lives on a finite periodic box
$[0,L]^d$, sampled on a grid of $N^d$ points with spacing $\Delta
x=L/N$. Periodicity discretizes the allowed wavenumbers to integer
multiples of the fundamental $k_F=2\pi/L$; finite sampling caps each
component at the Nyquist wavenumber $k_{\rm Ny}=\pi/\Delta x$. The
allowed mode set is therefore
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf k = \frac{2\pi}{L}\,\mathbf n,
\qquad
\mathbf n\in\mathbb Z^d,\;|n_a|\le N/2 \text{ for each axis },
\end{equation*}
giving $N^d$ independent modes per axis and $N^d$ total. This range
is the \emph{first Brillouin zone}: the largest set of distinct
wavenumbers a grid of spacing $\Delta x$ can resolve. The reason
modes outside this zone are not independent is that the grid only
samples the field at $x=j\Delta x$ for integer $j$, where
\begin{equation*}
e^{i(k+2\pi/\Delta x)\,j\Delta x}
  = e^{ikj\Delta x}\,e^{i 2\pi j}
  = e^{ikj\Delta x},
\end{equation*}
since $e^{i2\pi j}=1$ for all integers $j$. So a wave of wavenumber
$k+2\pi/\Delta x$ takes exactly the same value as a wave of
wavenumber $k$ at every grid point: the two modes are
\emph{indistinguishable} on the lattice. Any mode outside the first
Brillouin zone is therefore aliased onto a unique mode inside it,
shifted by some integer multiple of $2\pi/\Delta x$. This is the
same mechanism behind the alias sum (\ref{eq:Pk-alias}): when we
restrict $\delta_{2N}$ to the coarse grid we lose the ability to
distinguish those shifted modes, and their power gets folded onto
the in-zone partner. The name \emph{Brillouin zone}
comes from solid-state physics (the unit cell of the reciprocal
lattice); on a periodic grid it plays the same role as the
``fundamental period'' on a single 1D circle. Use the same physics convention but with the
Plancherel-friendly normalization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ft-discrete}
  \hat\delta(\mathbf k)
  = \Delta x^d\sum_{\mathbf x}\delta(\mathbf x)\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x},
  \qquad
  \delta(\mathbf x)
  = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf k}\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,e^{+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x},
\end{equation}
where $V=L^d$ and the sums run over the $N^d$ real-space points and
$\mathbf k$-modes respectively. Repeat the correlator computation:
\begin{align}
  \bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  &= \Delta x^{2d}\sum_{\mathbf x,\mathbf x'}
     \xi(\mathbf x'-\mathbf x)\,
     e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'} \notag\\
  &= \Delta x^{2d}\sum_{\mathbf x}e^{-i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf x}
     \sum_{\mathbf r}\xi(\mathbf r)\,e^{i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf r}.
\end{align}
The first sum is the lattice version of the delta function:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta x^d\sum_{\mathbf x}e^{-i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf x}
  = V\,\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'},
\end{equation*}
where $\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}$ is the Kronecker delta on the
allowed mode lattice. The second sum is the discrete analogue of
(\ref{eq:P-xi-FT}),
\begin{equation*}
  P(\mathbf k) \;\equiv\; \Delta x^d\sum_{\mathbf r}\xi(\mathbf r)\,
    e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}.
\end{equation*}
Combining the two,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pk-discrete}
  \boxed{\;\bigl\langle\hat\delta(\mathbf k)\,\hat\delta^*(\mathbf k')\bigr\rangle
  = V\,\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'}\,P(\mathbf k)\;}
\end{equation}
which is the box version of (\ref{eq:Pk-def}). Setting $\mathbf
k'=\mathbf k$ and using $\xi(\mathbf r)=\xi(-\mathbf r)$,
\begin{equation*}
\bigl\langle|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2\bigr\rangle
  = \Delta x^{2d}\,\frac{V}{\Delta x^d}\,\sum_{\mathbf r}\xi(\mathbf r)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}
   = V\,P(\mathbf k),
\end{equation*}
recovering the variance form $P(\mathbf k) =
\langle|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle/V$ quoted earlier.

\subsection*{Physical meaning}

Three complementary ways to read $P(\mathbf k)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Variance per mode.} $P(\mathbf k)$ is the typical
squared amplitude of the density wave with wavevector $\mathbf k$,
with the box volume factored out (eq.~\ref{eq:Pk-discrete}).

\item \textbf{Variance per logarithmic $k$-band.} The total variance
of the field decomposes by Wiener--Khinchin as
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma^2 \equiv \langle\delta^2(\mathbf x)\rangle
   = \xi(\mathbf 0)
   = \int\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,P(\mathbf k)
   = \int_0^\infty\frac{dk}{k}\,\Delta^2(k),
\end{equation*}
with the dimensionless $\Delta^2(k)\equiv k^d
P(k)/(2\pi)^d \times S_{d-1}$ ($S_{d-1}$ the unit-sphere surface
area), so $\Delta^2(k)$ is the contribution to the variance per
logarithmic $k$-interval. Equivalently, $\Delta^2(k)\sim 1$ marks
the scale at which the field has order-unity fluctuations --- the
non-linear scale.

\item \textbf{Fourier transform of the correlation function} (eq.\
\ref{eq:P-xi-FT}). Power on scale $k$ encodes correlation at
separation $r\sim 1/k$: a peak in $P(k)$ at some $k_0$ corresponds
to coherent overdensities and underdensities separated by $\sim
2\pi/k_0$ in real space (e.g.\ the BAO peak).
\end{itemize}

In simulations one estimates $P(\mathbf k)$ from a single realization
by replacing the ensemble average with a mode-by-mode squared
amplitude,
\begin{equation*}
  \widehat P(\mathbf k) = \frac{|\hat\delta(\mathbf k)|^2}{V},
\end{equation*}
and bins radially in $|\mathbf k|$; the variance of $\widehat
P(\mathbf k)$ at a single mode is $P(\mathbf k)$ itself (Gaussian
fields), so binning over $N_{\rm modes}$ in a shell reduces the
fractional uncertainty to $\sim 1/\sqrt{N_{\rm modes}}$.
