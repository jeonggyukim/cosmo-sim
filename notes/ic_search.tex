\title{Selecting an Initial-Conditions Seed on the Power Spectrum\\
       of a Subvolume}

\begin{abstract}
A subvolume of a simulation box does not measure the linear power spectrum. Its
estimator is unbiased for the theory convolved with the subvolume's own window,
which for the geometry used here lies $35\%$ below the theory at the largest
scale it can measure. Choosing an initial-conditions seed because its subvolume
spectrum matches
the unconvolved theory therefore selects realisations whose large-scale power is
anomalously high, by construction rather than by accident. We measure what this
does using $83\,542$ independent realisations at $N = 128^3$,
$L = 700\Mpch$. Selection displaces the subvolume's large-scale power by
$1.26\sigma$ and every other property by its correlation with that power times
$1.26$: the tidal shear by $0.62\sigma$, the cosmic-web composition by
$0.35\sigma$, the mean density, whose correlation is $0.001$, not at all. The
displacement saturates, because the realisation scatter bounds how far the power
can be pushed, so a search over a million seeds is no worse than one over a
thousand. Two controls confirm the cause: a randomly permuted criterion produces
$0.000 \pm 0.045$ in every property, and selecting against the convolved theory,
which asks for nothing atypical, produces $0.016$. Fitting an amplitude with the
window in the model returns $A = 0.9995 \pm 0.0765$ over all subvolumes with no
search at all, while the selected subvolumes have $A = 1.065$. The result is not
specific to power spectra: any rule for choosing which region to resimulate
conditions the field, and the correlation between the rule and the quantity of
interest predicts the bias in advance.
\end{abstract}

\section{The proposal}
\label{sec:proposal}

A cosmological simulation begins from one realisation of a random field. The
initial-conditions code draws a set of independent random numbers, called a
white noise field, from a stream fixed by an integer called the \emph{seed}, and
shapes that field so that it has the power spectrum linear theory predicts.
Changing the seed gives a different universe with the same statistics, in the
same way that rolling a die again gives a different number from the same
distribution.

Often only a small part of the box is of scientific interest: one halo, one
filament, one region to be rerun at much higher resolution. That second run is
called a \emph{zoom} simulation, and the region it refines has to be chosen in
advance, from the low-resolution box.

The proposal examined here is a rule for making that choice. Generate initial
conditions with many random seeds. In each, measure the power spectrum inside
the subvolume that will be resimulated. Adopt the seed whose subvolume spectrum
best matches linear theory, on the grounds that such a region is a faithful
piece of the universe to zoom into.

The intent is reasonable, and the failure is not obvious from the outside. This
note sets out why the criterion cannot do what it is meant to do, measures how
large the resulting distortion is, and shows that the distortion is bounded and
predictable.

Two conventions hold throughout. Write $L$ for the side of the periodic
simulation box and $N$ for the number of grid cells along each side, so the box
holds $N^3$ cells of side $L/N$; here $L = 700\Mpch$ and $N = 128$. The
subvolume is a \emph{pencil}: one eighth of the box across in two directions and
its full length in the third, so $87.5 \times 87.5 \times 700\ (\Mpch)^3$. The
name is descriptive; the shape is long and thin, and it is the shape a survey's
line-of-sight volume tends to have. The fields are Lagrangian densities
$\delta(q)$ at $z = 200$, generated by monofonIC, a cosmological
initial-conditions code that evaluates Lagrangian perturbation theory on a grid,
run here to second order (2LPT). Lagrangian means that
$\delta$ is written as a function of a particle's undisplaced starting position
$q$ on the grid, rather than of its displaced position; at $z = 200$ the two
differ by little, and nothing in this note depends on the distinction.

\section{A subvolume does not measure the theory}
\label{sec:window}

Measuring anything inside a region rather than in the whole box means throwing
away everything outside it. The clean way to write that is to multiply the field
by a \emph{mask} $W(\bx)$, a function equal to $1$ inside the region and $0$
outside it, and then to treat $W\delta$ as the field being measured. The
measurement sees $W\delta$ and nothing else.

Now recall a standard property of the Fourier transform: a product of two
functions in configuration space transforms into a convolution of their
transforms. Writing $\widetilde W(\bk)$ for the transform of the mask and
$\widetilde\delta(\bk)$ for that of the field, the transform of the masked field
is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:masked}
  \widetilde{(W\delta)}(\bk) \;=\; \int \! d^3k' \; \widetilde W(\bk - \bk')\,
  \widetilde\delta(\bk') .
\end{equation}

The power spectrum is the average of $|\widetilde{(W\delta)}|^2$ over
realisations. Take Eq.~\eqref{eq:masked}, multiply it by its own complex
conjugate, and average. Because the conjugate carries its own integration
variable, call it $\bk''$, the result is a double integral:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:double}
  \avg{|\widetilde{(W\delta)}(\bk)|^2} \;=\;
  \int \! d^3k' \int \! d^3k'' \;
  \widetilde W(\bk - \bk')\, \widetilde W^{*}(\bk - \bk'')\;
  \avg{\widetilde\delta(\bk')\,\widetilde\delta^{*}(\bk'')} .
\end{equation}
Everything outside the angle brackets is fixed by the geometry, so the only
factor that needs averaging is the last one.

Here the assumption of statistical homogeneity is used. It says that different
Fourier modes of the field are uncorrelated, which in an equation is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:homog}
  \avg{\widetilde\delta(\bk')\,\widetilde\delta^{*}(\bk'')} \;=\;
  \delta_{\rm D}(\bk' - \bk'')\; P_{\rm th}(\bk') ,
\end{equation}
with $\delta_{\rm D}$ the Dirac delta. This is the definition of the power
spectrum: the average vanishes for $\bk' \neq \bk''$, and for $\bk' = \bk''$ it
gives $P_{\rm th}$.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:homog} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:double}, the delta function
sets $\bk'' = \bk'$ and removes the second integral:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:conv}
  \avg{\hat P(\bk)} \;=\; \int \! d^3k' \; |\widetilde W(\bk - \bk')|^2\,
  P_{\rm th}(\bk')
  \;=\; \big[P_{\rm th} \ast |\widetilde W|^2\big](\bk)
  \;\neq\; P_{\rm th}(\bk),
\end{equation}
where $\ast$ denotes convolution, $\hat P$ is the spectrum the region measures,
and $P_{\rm th}$ is the power spectrum linear theory predicts for the unmasked
field. The quantity $|\widetilde W|^2$ is called the \emph{window function} of
the region.

In words, Eq.~\eqref{eq:conv} says that the spectrum measured at a wavenumber
$\bk$ is not $P_{\rm th}(\bk)$ but a weighted average of $P_{\rm th}$ over
neighbouring wavenumbers, with $|\widetilde W|^2$ supplying the weights. The
width of that averaging is set by the size of the region: the smaller the
region, the broader $|\widetilde W|^2$ is and the wider the range of $P_{\rm th}$
that gets mixed in. For the pencil, \S\ref{sec:winform} shows that width is
$\Delta k_\perp = 0.072\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$.

Whether that average comes out above or below $P_{\rm th}(\bk)$ depends on the
shape of the spectrum, so it is worth following through with numbers. Two
things combine. First, the amount of $k$-space lying a distance $q$ from the
centre of the window grows with $q$, so the average is weighted towards the
outer part of the range rather than towards the centre. Second, the linear
spectrum used here peaks at $k_{\rm eq} = 0.020\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, the
wavenumber that entered the horizon at matter--radiation equality, and falls
steeply above it: by $k = \Delta k_\perp$ it has dropped to $38\%$ of its value
at the largest scale the pencil can measure.

Putting those together: when the window is centred on the largest scale, it
reaches out to wavenumbers where the theory is two and a half times smaller, and
most of the $k$-space it covers lies there. The average is therefore pulled well
below the theory at that scale. Measured, the ratio at the first bin is $0.65$.

\subsection{The window of a pencil}
\label{sec:winform}

The window can be written down exactly. Take one axis first, and fix two indices
that run along it. In configuration space, $j = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$ labels the
grid cells, and the mask is $1$ for $j = 0, \dots, n-1$ and $0$ elsewhere. In
Fourier space, $m = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$ labels the modes the grid can represent,
and carries the physical wavenumber
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mdef}
  k \;=\; \frac{2\pi m}{L} ,
\end{equation}
so $m = 1$ is the box fundamental and $m = N/2$ is the Nyquist mode. On a
periodic grid the discrete transform of the mask is then a sum of $n$ terms,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:geo}
  \widetilde W(m) \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} e^{-2\pi i\, jm/N}
  \;=\; \frac{1 - e^{-2\pi i\, mn/N}}{1 - e^{-2\pi i\, m/N}} ,
\end{equation}
where the second step sums a geometric series whose ratio is
$e^{-2\pi i m/N}$: each step in $j$ multiplies by that same factor. To turn that ratio of complex numbers into something real,
use $1 - e^{-i\theta} = e^{-i\theta/2}\,(e^{i\theta/2} - e^{-i\theta/2})
= e^{-i\theta/2}\,2i\sin(\theta/2)$ on the numerator and the denominator
separately, with $\theta = 2\pi mn/N$ above and $\theta = 2\pi m/N$ below. The
factors of $2i$ cancel and the exponentials combine into one phase,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dirichlet}
  \widetilde W(m) \;=\; e^{-i\pi m (n-1)/N}\,
  \frac{\sin(\pi m n/N)}{\sin(\pi m/N)} .
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{Dirichlet kernel}, the periodic counterpart of $\sin x/x$.
Taking the modulus squared discards the phase.

The mask is a product of one such run per axis, so its transform factorises and
the three axes simply multiply. Writing $\mathbf{m} = (m_1, m_2, m_3)$ for the
integer wavevector, with $n_i$ cells masked along axis $i$, and dividing by
$N^3$ to match the normalisation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:conv},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:winform}
  \frac{|\widetilde W(\mathbf{m})|^2}{N^6} \;=\;
  \prod_{i=1}^{3}
  \left[ \frac{\sin(\pi m_i n_i / N)}{N \sin(\pi m_i / N)} \right]^2 .
\end{equation}
At $m_i = 0$ both sine functions vanish and the ratio must be taken as a limit:
for small $\theta$, $\sin\theta \to \theta$, so the factor tends to $n_i/N$. Two
features of Eq.~\eqref{eq:winform} matter later.

First, the pencil spans the whole box along its length, $n_z = N$, so the $z$
factor contains $\sin(\pi m_z)$ and vanishes for every integer $m_z \neq 0$. The
window is a Kronecker delta in that direction: the pencil does not smear its
long axis at all, and the entire deficit is transverse.

Second, the transverse factors reduce to $\mathrm{sinc}^2(k_i \ell_\perp/2)$ at
low $k$, with $\ell_\perp = L/8 = 87.5\Mpch$ the pencil width. The first zero
sits at
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dkperp}
  \Delta k_\perp \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{\ell_\perp} \;=\; 0.072\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1},
\end{equation}
which sets the width of the smearing. The box fundamental is
$k_f = 2\pi/L = 0.0090\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, eight times smaller, so the window
is eight times wider than the largest scale being measured. That is why the
deficit is tens of per cent and not a small correction.

The convolution is never evaluated directly, which would cost $N^6$ operations.
It is done in configuration space, where convolution becomes a product:
$P_{\rm th}$ transforms to $\xi$, $|\widetilde W|^2$ transforms to the mask
autocorrelation $(W \star W)(r)$, the two are multiplied, and the result is
transformed back. That same mask autocorrelation is what divides out of the
correlation-function estimator in \S\ref{sec:xi-unbiased}, which is exactly why
$\xi$ escapes the deficit and $P(k)$ does not.

Three things about this deficit are worth separating. It is deterministic: it is
the same in every realisation, since $W$ is fixed by the geometry and does not
depend on the random field. It therefore does not average away when more
realisations are added. And it is not noise, so it cannot be beaten down by
measuring more carefully. For the pencil geometry used here it is a $35\%$
deficit at the largest scale the region can measure, and it deepens to $39\%$
at $k_{\rm eq}$, where the theory turns over.

Equation~\eqref{eq:conv} is a statement about an ensemble average, so it can be
tested by averaging many measurements and comparing. Averaging $2\,005\,008$
subvolume measurements against the convolved curve gives a ratio of
$0.9996 \pm 0.0001$, and averaging the full-box measurements against the
unconvolved theory gives $0.99994 \pm 0.00002$. The estimator is unbiased for
the convolved theory and biased against the raw theory, exactly as the equation
says.

The measurement is therefore not failing to reach the theory. It is succeeding
at measuring something else: the theory as seen through a window of that size
and shape.

\subsection{The correlation function carries no such deficit}
\label{sec:xi-unbiased}

The two-point correlation function $\xi(r)$ is the other standard way to
describe the clustering of a field. It measures the excess probability, over
what an unclustered random distribution of points would give, of finding two
points separated by a distance $r$. Formally it is the ensemble average
$\xi(r) = \avg{\delta(\bx)\,\delta(\bx + \mathbf{r})}$, which for a
statistically homogeneous and isotropic field depends only on the separation
$r$, not on the position $\bx$ and not on the direction of $\mathbf{r}$. A
positive $\xi(r)$ means matter is more clustered at that separation than a
random field would be, and a negative $\xi(r)$ means it is more evenly spread.
It carries the same information as $P(k)$, being its Fourier transform.

The same mask leaves $\xi(r)$ alone, and the contrast with the power spectrum is
worth stating because it decides what a seed search is really doing.

The correlation function is a ratio of pair counts. With the same mask $W$ as
above, the estimator evaluated on a grid is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:xi-est}
  \hat\xi(r) \;=\;
  \frac{\sum_x W_x W_{x+r}\, \delta_x \delta_{x+r}}
       {\sum_x W_x W_{x+r}} ,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over grid cells $x$ and the product $W_x W_{x+r}$ is $1$ only
when both ends of the pair lie inside the region. This is the Landy--Szalay
estimator, the standard form $(DD - 2DR + RR)/RR$ used for survey correlation
functions, in which $DD$, $DR$ and $RR$ count data--data, data--random and
random--random pairs; on a grid the $RR$ count is the denominator above and can
be evaluated exactly rather than by generating a random catalogue. The
equivalence is worked through in the companion note
\texttt{xi\_estimators.tex}, which also shows that the direct lag sum and the
zero-padded FFT compute the same estimator.

The mask appears in the numerator and in the denominator in the same way, and
that is what makes the estimator unbiased. Taking the expectation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi-est}, the factor $\avg{\delta_x \delta_{x+r}} = \xi(r)$ is the
same for every pair separated by $r$, so it comes out of the sum, and the two
mask terms then cancel exactly:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:xi-unbiased}
  \avg{\hat\xi(r)} \;=\; \xi(r)
  \qquad\text{for any mask } W .
\end{equation}
No window enters. A subvolume measures the true $\xi$, however small the
subvolume is and however few pairs survive at a given separation.

The asymmetry is between a product and a ratio. Both statistics start from the
same masked field $W\delta$. The power spectrum is the transform of that
product, and a product transforms to a convolution, which mixes wavenumbers and
cannot be undone mode by mode. The correlation function divides by the mask's
own pair count at the same lag, and division undoes multiplication.

Measured on $19\,300$ realisations of $24$ subvolumes each, the mean of
$463\,200$ subvolume correlation functions is $1.015$ times the linear theory
over $r < 88\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$, against $1.008$ for the full box: both consistent
with Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi-unbiased}, and neither needing a window correction. The
same subvolumes in the same realisations measure a power spectrum $35\%$ below
the theory at the largest scale they reach.

One practical warning follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi-est}, and
\texttt{xi\_estimators.tex} makes it at length: the denominator must be the
mask's own pair count at each lag. Dividing instead by the region's volume, a
constant, reintroduces a deficit that looks like the power-spectrum one and is
purely an error of normalisation.

\section{What the search selects}
\label{sec:selects}

If the mean measurement lies $35\%$ below a curve, the only realisations that
reach that curve are those with anomalously high power. A search for a matching
seed does not find a region that agrees with the theory. It finds a region that
is unusual in precisely the way needed to cancel a geometric deficit.

The cleanest way to see this is to stop searching and fit instead. For each
subvolume, fit the amplitude $A$ in $\hat P(k) = A\,P_{\rm model}(k)$, which asks
how much the model curve has to be scaled up or down to match the measurement.
The fit is done by generalised least squares, meaning that the residuals at
different wavenumbers are weighted by the inverse of their covariance matrix
rather than treated as independent; that covariance is measured from the
ensemble of subvolumes itself. The fit is run twice, once with the window in the
model and once without. Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} gives the result and
Figure~\ref{fig:amplitude} shows the distributions.

Put the window in the model and no search is needed: the fit returns $A = 1$.
The search instead keeps the realisations that make a misspecified model return
$A = 1$, and those realisations have $A = 1.065$ when measured correctly. An
amplitude of $1.065 \pm 0.077$ would not be reported as agreement with a
cosmology; the search selects for exactly that outcome and reports it as
agreement.

One caution about the $0.85\sigma$ in the last row of
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}. It is the deviation of the fitted amplitude from
unity in units of the fit uncertainty $\pm 0.077$, which is the scatter of the
fitted amplitude across all subvolumes. That is a different statistic from the
$1.26\sigma$ used everywhere else in this note, which is the shift of the
large-scale power ratio in units of the region-to-region scatter of that ratio,
over the narrower band $k \le 2\Delta k_\perp$ defined in
\S\ref{sec:quantities}. The two describe the same selected sample and agree in
sign and rough size, but they are not the same number and should not be
reconciled with each other.

\section{What this does to every other property}
\label{sec:conditioning}

So far the argument concerns only the power spectrum, which is the quantity the
criterion looks at. But a region that will be resimulated has many other
properties, and those are usually what the science depends on: how strongly it
is being sheared by its surroundings, whether it sits in a knot or a void, how
fast it is moving. This section shows that those properties move too, and that
how far each moves is fixed in advance by a single number.

Doing that requires first saying precisely what is measured, which is
\S\ref{sec:quantities}, and then applying a selection to each measurement, which
is \S\ref{sec:each}.

\subsection{The quantities measured}
\label{sec:quantities}

Each quantity below is reduced to a single number per subvolume, so that a
selection can be asked what it does to that number. Two conventions are used
throughout. Angle brackets $\avg{\cdot}_V$ denote an average over the cells of
the subvolume, and $\delta_R$ is the density contrast smoothed with a Gaussian of
radius $R$. The transverse width $\ell_\perp = L/8 = 87.5\Mpch$ and the
wavenumber it sets, $\Delta k_\perp = 2\pi/\ell_\perp =
0.072\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, are those of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dkperp}. The Nyquist
wavenumber of the grid is $k_{\rm Ny} = \pi N / L$, the largest wavenumber a
grid of spacing $L/N$ can represent.

\begin{description}
\item[The criterion.] The distance of a measured subvolume spectrum
  $\hat P(k)$ from a reference curve $P_{\rm ref}(k)$, over the band the
  selection uses:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:criterion}
    D \;=\; \left[ \frac{1}{N_{\rm bins}}
      \sum_{k \,\le\, 2\Delta k_\perp}
      \ln^2 \frac{\hat P(k)}{P_{\rm ref}(k)} \right]^{1/2} .
  \end{equation}
  $N_{\rm bins}$ is the number of bins in the band, so $D$ is a root-mean-square
  logarithmic deviation; the logarithm makes it sensitive to fractional rather
  than absolute differences.

  The proposal takes $P_{\rm ref} = P_{\rm th}$, the raw linear theory. The
  control of \S\ref{sec:controls} takes
  $P_{\rm ref} = P_{\rm th} \ast |\widetilde W|^2$ instead. A smaller $D$ is a
  closer match, and a selection keeps the subvolumes with the smallest $D$.

\item[Large-scale power.] The mean ratio of the measured spectrum to the
  convolved theory over the same band the criterion uses,
  $\avg{\hat P / (P_{\rm th} \ast |\widetilde W|^2)}_{k \le 2\Delta k_\perp}$.
  It is quoted against the convolved curve so that a value of one means an
  ordinary region, not a matching one.

\item[Small-scale power.] The same ratio over
  $2\Delta k_\perp < k \le 0.9\,k_{\rm Ny}$, that is, the scales the criterion
  does not look at.

\item[Tidal tensor.] $T_{ij} = \partial_i \partial_j \Phi$ with
  $\nabla^2 \Phi = \delta_R$, where $\Phi$ is normalised so that $\delta_R$
  sources it directly, absorbing the usual $4\pi G \bar\rho a^2$. With
  $\partial_i \to i k_i$ and $\nabla^2 \to -k^2$, Poisson's equation gives
  $\Phi(\bk) = -\delta_R(\bk)/k^2$ and the two minus signs cancel:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:tidal}
    T_{ij}(\bk) \;=\; \frac{k_i k_j}{k^2}\, \delta_R(\bk) .
  \end{equation}
  Its trace is $\delta_R$, and its eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge
  \lambda_3$ describe how the region is stretched or compressed along three
  orthogonal axes.

\item[Tidal shear.] The part of the tidal tensor that changes a region's shape
  rather than its volume, obtained by removing the trace,
  $s_{ij} = T_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{3}\delta_R \delta_{ij}$. The number quoted is its
  amplitude within the subvolume,
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:shear}
    s \;=\; \Big\langle s_{ij}s_{ij} \Big\rangle_V^{1/2}
      \;=\; \Big\langle T_{ij}T_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{3}\delta_R^2 \Big\rangle_V^{1/2}.
  \end{equation}
  The ratio $\avg{s_{ij}s_{ij}} / \avg{\delta_R^2} = \tfrac{2}{3}$ exactly, for
  any field. Contracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidal} with itself gives
  $\sum_{ij} |T_{ij}|^2 = (\sum_i k_i^2)(\sum_j k_j^2)\,|\delta_R|^2/k^4 =
  |\delta_R|^2$, since $\sum_i k_i^2 = k^2$, so
  $\avg{T_{ij}T_{ij}}_V = \avg{\delta_R^2}_V$ by Parseval and removing the trace
  leaves $1 - \tfrac{1}{3}$. The measurement reproduces $0.6667$, which is
  therefore a check on the code and not on any assumption about the field.

\item[Mean density.] $\bar\delta_R = \avg{\delta_R}_V$, the smoothed density
  contrast averaged over the subvolume.

\item[Cosmic-web fractions.] Each cell is classified by how many eigenvalues of
  $T_{ij}$ are positive, a positive eigenvalue meaning collapse along that axis:
  three is a knot, two a filament, one a sheet, none a void. The four numbers
  quoted are the fractions of the subvolume's cells in each class, and they sum
  to one by construction \citep{Hahn2007}. Unlike the shear ratio above, these
  fractions do depend on the field being Gaussian: the eigenvalue distribution
  of \citet{Doroshkevich1970} gives $0.08$, $0.42$, $0.42$, $0.08$ independently
  of the shape of $P(k)$, and the measurement reproduces $0.0799$, $0.4198$,
  $0.4202$, $0.0801$.

\item[Ellipticity.] The shape of the tidal ellipsoid,
  $e = (\lambda_1 - \lambda_3)/2L$ with $L = (\sum_i \lambda_i^2)^{1/2}$. The
  usual definition divides by the trace $\delta_R$, which passes through zero
  for a region of average density and makes the ratio diverge; dividing by the
  norm keeps the same meaning and stays finite.

\item[Bulk flow.] $|\avg{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_V|$, where the Zel'dovich
  displacement $\boldsymbol\Psi(\bk) = i\,\bk\,\delta(\bk)/k^2$ is the gradient
  of the same potential that sources the density field (first-order Lagrangian
  perturbation theory, derived in \texttt{cosmo\_ic.tex}). The vector is
  averaged first and the magnitude taken second, so this depends on the phases
  of the modes and not only on their amplitudes.

\item[Environment contrast.] The subvolume's mean density minus that of the
  eight subvolumes surrounding it, which asks whether the region sits in an
  overdense neighbourhood rather than whether it is overdense relative to the
  whole box.
\end{description}

Smoothing radii of $R = 20$ and $40\Mpch$ are used throughout. The first is
comfortably inside the subvolume, which is $87.5\Mpch$ across; the second is
half that width, so the smoothed field near the subvolume's faces is built
partly from material outside it, and the $R = 40\Mpch$ numbers describe the
region together with its surroundings rather than the region alone.

\subsection{The effect of selection on each}
\label{sec:each}

A region has many properties besides its power spectrum, and the criterion
mentions none of them. They move regardless, and by an amount fixed in advance.

Selection acts on one quantity, which here is the subvolume's large-scale power.
Call it $x$, and call any other property $y$. If $x$ and $y$ are jointly
Gaussian with means $\bar x, \bar y$, standard deviations $\sigma_x, \sigma_y$
and correlation coefficient $\rho$, then a standard result of Gaussian
statistics gives the mean of $y$ among all realisations with a given value of
$x$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:condmean}
  \avg{y \mid x} \;=\; \bar y + \rho\,\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x}\,(x - \bar x).
\end{equation}
Knowing $x$ therefore tells you something about $y$, and how much is controlled
entirely by $\rho$.

It is convenient to measure each displacement in units of that quantity's own
scatter, because then quantities with different physical units can be compared.
Subtract $\bar y$ from both sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:condmean} and divide through
by $\sigma_y$. The left side becomes the displacement of $y$ in units of
$\sigma_y$, and the factor $(x - \bar x)/\sigma_x$ on the right is already the
displacement of $x$ in units of $\sigma_x$. Writing
$\Delta_y = (\avg{y \mid x} - \bar y)/\sigma_y$ and
$\Delta_x = (x - \bar x)/\sigma_x$, the relation reduces to
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho}
  \Delta_y \;=\; \rho\,\Delta_x ,
\end{equation}
and to nothing else. The tightness of the selection, the shape of the criterion,
the number of seeds searched: none of them appear. They enter only through the
single number $\Delta_x$, and once $\Delta_x$ is known, every other property's
shift follows from its correlation with $x$ alone.

Here the selection displaces the subvolume's large-scale power by
$\Delta_x = 1.26\sigma$, so every other property should move by
$\rho \times 1.26$. Table~\ref{tab:rho} shows that it does.

\section{The distortion is bounded}
\label{sec:bounded}

An obvious worry is that searching harder makes matters worse without limit. It
does not, and the reason follows from the same identity.

The reason is in the form of the criterion. Equation~\eqref{eq:criterion}
measures distance from a fixed target curve, not height above it, so a subvolume
that overshoots is scored as badly as one that undershoots. Once the search
finds realisations whose power reaches the raw theory, searching further finds
ones that match more closely, not ones with more power. The displacement
saturates near $1.3\sigma$, and by Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho} everything else saturates
at $\rho \times 1.3$.

Contrast an idealised selection acting directly on the power and keeping the
largest of $N$ draws. By extreme-value statistics that maximum grows as
$\sqrt{2\ln N}$, slowly but without bound, reaching $4.9\sigma$ at a million
draws against the $1.3\sigma$ measured here.

the ratio for the tidal shear stays between $0.43$ and $0.50$ against
$\rho = 0.451$. Nothing in the conclusion depends on where the threshold is
placed.

In plain terms: the criterion biases the ensemble by a fraction of its natural
spread. A single selected region is not physically extreme and usually overlaps
the ordinary population. What is not defensible is calling it typical.

\section{Two controls}
\label{sec:controls}

Both competing explanations can be excluded by measurement rather than argument.

\emph{Is the measurement procedure producing shifts on its own?} Replace the
criterion with a random permutation of itself, so that the same number of
subvolumes is kept but which ones are kept has nothing to do with their spectra,
and keep everything else identical. Every property returns $0.000 \pm 0.045$.
The geometry, the estimator and the selection arithmetic add no shift of their
own.

\emph{Is it the act of selecting, or the curve being matched?} Change only the
target: select against the theory convolved with the window, which is the curve
the estimator is unbiased for. That target requires nothing atypical, so it
should do nothing.

\section{How many independent statements these are}
\label{sec:independence}

Tables~\ref{tab:rho} and~\ref{tab:controls} list several properties, which
invites reading them as several independent confirmations. They are fewer than
they look, and the correlation matrix says how many.

The four web fractions sum to unity at every cell, so the correlation matrix has
two exactly vanishing eigenvalues, one per smoothing radius, and the two radii
largely repeat each other: the shear at $R = 20$ and $R = 40\Mpch$ correlates at
$0.76$. Count the independent directions with the \emph{participation ratio},
built from the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of the correlation matrix,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:participation}
  N_{\rm eff} \;=\; \frac{\big(\sum_i \lambda_i\big)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2},
\end{equation}
which equals $n$ if the $n$ quantities are uncorrelated and $1$ if they are all
copies of one. The fifteen quantities here are the two power ratios, and the
shear, mean density and four web fractions at each of two radii, plus the bulk
flow. Three eigenvalues exceed unity and $N_{\rm eff} = 3.49$.

The three surviving directions are interpretable, and this is the useful part.
The shear correlates with the web fractions at between $-0.01$ and $-0.09$, so
the two are nearly orthogonal to each other while both correlate with the
selected power: two genuinely independent confirmations rather than a dozen
correlated ones. The bulk flow is the third direction, and does not move.

\section{The same argument applies to any selection rule}
\label{sec:general}

Equation~\eqref{eq:rho} says nothing about power spectra. It governs any rule
for choosing which region to resimulate.

A region chosen for a quiet merger history, for a particular halo mass, for an
isolated environment, or for resembling an observed system, is a region on which
a condition has been imposed. Every property correlated with that condition is
displaced, by the same arithmetic, whether or not the displacement was intended
and whether or not it is reported. The choice of region is part of the numerical
experiment and belongs in its interpretation, in the same way a selection
function belongs in the interpretation of a survey.

The useful half is that this is predictable before anything is run. The
correlations are properties of the field and the geometry, not of the selection,
and a few hundred realisations measure them. Anyone proposing a criterion can be
told in advance which properties of the resimulated region it will distort and
by how much.

The converse is equally useful. A property uncorrelated with the criterion is
untouched: the mean density of the region has $\rho = 0.001$ with the subvolume's
large-scale power and shows no shift at any search size or tolerance. A criterion
is safe for exactly those quantities it does not correlate with, and the
correlation matrix identifies them.

\section{Scoring a region for typicality}
\label{sec:typicality}

If a region can be chosen badly, can it be chosen well: not for matching a
curve, but for being ordinary in many properties at once? The obvious attempt
fails, and instructively.

\subsection{The obvious score, and why it is wrong}
\label{sec:blandness}

Write $\Delta_Q$ for the displacement of a quantity $Q$ in units of its own
scatter over all subvolumes, which is the same $\Delta$ as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho}:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:deltaQ}
  \Delta_Q \;=\; \frac{Q - \bar Q}{\sigma_Q} ,
\end{equation}
with $\bar Q$ the mean over all subvolumes and $\sigma_Q$ their standard
deviation. Collect the $\Delta_Q$ of $d$ measured quantities into a vector
$\boldsymbol\Delta$ and score a region by its squared length,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:naive}
  S_{\rm naive} \;=\; \sum_{Q} \Delta_Q^2 ,
\end{equation}
keeping the smallest: the region closest to the mean in everything at once.

That rule does not select a typical region. It selects an unusually featureless
one. For a single Gaussian variable the most likely value is the mean, but for
$d$ of them almost no realisation is near the origin, because the volume at
radius $r$ grows as $r^{d-1}$ and beats the falling density until
$r \approx \sqrt{d}$. The probability mass sits in a shell at $\sqrt{d}$. With
$d = 13$, a random region has $\sum_Q \Delta_Q^2 \approx 13$, and a region at
$0$ is as unusual as one at $26$. Minimising chooses an outlier, in the one
direction nobody checks.

\subsection{Two corrections}
\label{sec:mahalanobis}

Equation~\eqref{eq:naive} adds the quantities as though independent, and
\S\ref{sec:independence} showed they are not: fifteen quantities span about
three independent directions, and the web fractions sum to unity so the
correlation matrix is singular. Whiten by the correlation matrix $\mathbf{C}$,
giving the squared Mahalanobis distance
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mahal}
  S_{\rm M} \;=\; \boldsymbol\Delta^{\rm T}\, \mathbf{C}^{-1}\,
                  \boldsymbol\Delta ,
\end{equation}
with the inverse taken on the non-degenerate subspace, of dimension
$d_{\rm eff} = 13$ here. For a Gaussian field $S_{\rm M}$ follows $\chi^2$ with
$d_{\rm eff}$ degrees of freedom, so its mean is $d_{\rm eff}$.

Then do not minimise it. Ask for a region at the typical distance,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shell}
  S_{\rm shell} \;=\; \big| S_{\rm M} - d_{\rm eff} \big| ,
\end{equation}
which keeps regions on the shell where the mass is rather than at the centre
where it is not.

\subsection{What each score actually does}
\label{sec:scores-measured}

Read the shift columns alone and both minimising rules look like successes: no
quantity moves by more than a quarter of its scatter, far better than the
$+1.27$ of the criterion this note is about. The spread columns show what that
reading misses. The kept regions retain only a third to a half of the natural
scatter in every quantity. They are not typical regions; they are regions with
much less internal structure than any real region has. A simulation started from
one of them would be smoother than the universe, in a way no table of means
would reveal.

Targeting the shell removes the problem. Every shift falls below $0.07\sigma$
and the retained spread is within three to eleven per cent of the parent. The
score, corrected in both ways, does what was wanted of it.

\subsection{But a random draw already does this}
\label{sec:random}

The last row of Table~\ref{tab:typicality} decides the matter. A seed drawn at
random, with no score and no search, shifts every quantity by less than
$0.02\sigma$ and retains the scatter to within two per cent. It beats the best
score, for a reason no work on the score can overcome: a random draw is unbiased
by construction, for every quantity, including the ones nobody thought to
measure. So the recommendation for a region intended to be representative is to
take a random seed. Every criterion added is a condition imposed on the field,
and \S\ref{sec:general} says every condition displaces everything correlated
with it.

The score is nonetheless worth computing, in two situations.

\emph{As a diagnostic.} When a region has been chosen for any reason, compute
$S_{\rm M}$ and the individual $\Delta_Q$ for it and report them. A region with
$S_{\rm M}$ near $d_{\rm eff}$ and small individual displacements is
demonstrably ordinary; one with $S_{\rm M} = 30$ is not. This is
recommendation~4 of \S\ref{sec:recommendations} made into a number.

The two halves are not interchangeable, and the scalar alone is the tempting
thing to quote. A $\chi^2$ with $d_{\rm eff} = 13$ has standard deviation
$\sqrt{2 d_{\rm eff}} = 5.1$, so displacing one direction out of thirteen by
$1.27\sigma$ adds only $1.6$ to $S_{\rm M}$. Table~\ref{tab:typicality} bears
this out: the raw-theory selection sits at $S_{\rm M} = 13.7$ against $13.0$ for
a random region, a difference of $0.14$ standard deviations. The scalar score is
almost blind to the bias this note is about, because it asks whether a region is
at the right \emph{distance} from the mean while this selection changes only the
\emph{direction}. $S_{\rm M}$ catches the failure of \S\ref{sec:blandness},
where the distance collapses to $3.3$; the individual $\Delta_Q$ catch the
failure of \S\ref{sec:selects}, where it does not move. Report both.

\emph{As a tie-break under a constraint.} Often the science requires a
particular region: a halo of a given mass, a protocluster, a void. That
constraint cannot be avoided and should not be, but it usually leaves many
candidates. Among them, Eq.~\eqref{eq:shell} picks one that is unremarkable in
everything the constraint does not fix. The constraint's own bias remains and
must still be reported; what the score removes is the additional, unintended
bias that an arbitrary choice among the candidates would have added.

\subsection{Which quantities belong in the score}
\label{sec:which-Q}

The score is only as good as its list of quantities, and no list is complete. A
region can be typical in everything measured and unusual in something else. The
list should therefore be chosen from the science the simulation is for: include
the properties of the initial region that the intended measurement is sensitive
to.

Two rules make the choice concrete. First, include a quantity if the science
depends on it, even when it correlates with something already in the list; the
whitening in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mahal} handles the redundancy, so nothing is
double-counted. Second, choose the smoothing radii from the mass scale the
science concerns, using
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mass}
  M(R) \;=\; \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\Omega_m \rho_{\rm crit} R^3
  \;\approx\; 3.5\times10^{11}
  \left(\frac{R}{1\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h}\right)^3 h^{-1} M_\odot ,
\end{equation}
so that a radius is chosen to enclose the mass of the objects being studied
rather than for convenience.

\section{A related point: which redshift the transfer functions are taken at}
\label{sec:ztarget}

This section is not about selection. It records a property of the initial
conditions used here that anyone measuring the two matter components separately
will meet.

In the runs above the baryon and cold dark matter fields carry the same acoustic
oscillations, differing only by a smooth offset of a few tenths of a per cent.
One would expect them to differ, since before recombination the baryons are held
up by radiation pressure and the cold dark matter is not. That they do not is
not a defect of the transfer function code. It follows from \emph{back-scaling},
the default in monofonIC and most initial-conditions codes: rather than
evaluating the transfer functions at the starting redshift, the code evaluates
them at $z_{\rm target} = 0$ and divides by the linear growth factor to reach
the starting redshift. By $z = 0$ the baryons have long since fallen into the
cold dark matter potentials, so the two transfer functions have nearly converged
there, and one common growth factor preserves that near-equality.

Setting \texttt{[cosmology] ztarget} equal to \texttt{zstart} takes the transfer
functions at the starting redshift itself, where the baryons have not caught up,
and makes the back-scaling factor unity. Call this the \emph{forward}
convention. Measured at $N = 256^3$, $L = 700\Mpch$, $z = 200$, from the same
white noise, over $k = 0.02$--$0.5\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$:

shows that each reproduces the theory its own run writes. They promise different
things. The back-scaled field is constructed so that an $N$-body code carrying
no radiation and no baryon physics evolves it to the correct total matter field
at $z = 0$, but the split between the two components at the starting redshift is
then not the physical one. The forward field is the true field at the starting
redshift, with the physical split, and carries no such guarantee about $z = 0$.

For the measurements in this note the choice does not matter: they use the total
matter field, which is the same under both conventions up to the growth factor,
and both the window convolution and the conditioning identity are unaffected by
an overall normalisation. It matters only for someone comparing the two
components to each other.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Compare in the basis you measured in.} Convolve the theory with
  the subvolume's window and compare against that. It reproduces the measurement
  to a fraction of a per cent, and requires no search: a typical region already
  agrees with it.

\item \textbf{If a number is wanted, fit one.} Put the window in the model and
  fit an amplitude. It returns $A = 1.000 \pm 0.077$ with no seed selection at
  all, and the width of that interval is the honest statement of how well a
  region of this size constrains the amplitude.

\item \textbf{Do not treat agreement with the unwindowed theory as validation.}
  A region that agrees with it is, for that reason, not a representative region.

\item \textbf{If a region must be chosen on some criterion}, and often one must
  be, measure the correlation between that criterion and the properties the
  science depends on, and quote the induced shift with the result. That converts
  a hidden bias into a stated systematic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Realisations are generated with a monofonIC fork that writes the Lagrangian
density field $\delta(q)$ directly on the grid. The unmodified code would go on
to displace particles off that grid and assign them velocities, which this
analysis does not need, since every quantity here is read off the density field
itself. Each realisation is a $128^3$ grid in a $700\Mpch$ box at $z = 200$ with
second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory and no amplitude fixing, meaning
that the amplitude of each Fourier mode is drawn from its full Rayleigh
distribution rather than set to the ensemble-average value; realisation-to-realisation
scatter is therefore present at every scale, as it is in a real survey volume.
Twenty-four subvolumes are measured per realisation, drawn from a pool of $192$.
That pool comes about as follows: transverse to any one axis the box divides
into an $8 \times 8$ grid of non-overlapping pencils, and there are three
choices of which axis the pencil runs along, giving $8 \times 8 \times 3 = 192$.
Pencils in different orientations overlap one another, which is one reason the
bootstrap below resamples whole realisations rather than individual pencils.

The power spectrum of a subvolume is measured by masked fast Fourier transform
with the normalisation of \citet{Park1994}, and the theory is convolved with the
same window on the same grid, so measurement and prediction share every
convention.

The tidal tensor, the shear and the cosmic-web classification are defined in
\S\ref{sec:quantities}, together with the two analytic checks that validate this
implementation and the values it reproduces for them.

Uncertainties come from a bootstrap: the set of realisations is resampled with
replacement many times, the whole selection and shift calculation is redone on
each resampled set, and the scatter across those repeats is quoted as the
uncertainty. The resampling is over whole realisations, never over individual
subvolumes, because the subvolumes within one realisation share that
realisation's Fourier modes and are not independent draws. At the one per cent
tolerance the retained subvolumes come from $17\,835$ distinct realisations,
$1.12$ per realisation, so they are close to independent in any case.

under \texttt{scripts/ic\_search/}; the seed ranges and configuration of each
production run are recorded in \texttt{scripts/ic\_search/RUNS.md}.
\par}
